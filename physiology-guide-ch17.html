Chapter 17 • Respiratory System

Gas Exchange & Regulation of Breathing

How oxygen gets into your blood and why you can't stop breathing

🎯 What You'll Learn

  • How gases move between air and blood (it's all about pressure gradients)
  • Why hemoglobin is the MVP of oxygen transport
  • How CO₂ is transported (it's more clever than you'd think)
  • Why your brain automatically controls your breathing
  • What your body actually monitors to decide how fast to breathe

1The Big Picture: What Happens After You Inhale?

💡 The Big Idea

Gas exchange works by diffusion—gases naturally move from areas of HIGH concentration (pressure) to LOW concentration. Your body doesn't actively "pump" oxygen; it simply creates the right conditions for diffusion to happen automatically.

💭 Think of it like perfume in a room...

If you spray perfume in one corner of a room, the scent molecules will naturally spread throughout—from high concentration to low concentration. This is diffusion. The same principle moves oxygen from air (high O₂) into blood (lower O₂).

2Understanding Partial Pressures & Gas Laws

Pgas = % of gas × Ptotal
Dalton's Law: Each gas's partial pressure depends on what percentage of the mixture it makes up
PO₂ = 21% × 760 mmHg = ~160 mmHg

🔑 Henry's Law

The amount of gas that dissolves in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure. Higher partial pressure = more gas dissolves. This is why O₂ dissolves into blood in the lungs (high PO₂) and CO₂ dissolves out of blood (high PCO₂ in blood).

🔑 The Golden Rule of Gas Exchange

Gases diffuse from HIGH partial pressure → LOW partial pressure. Always. No exceptions.

Partial Pressures at Different Locations

Atmospheric air PO₂160 mmHg
Alveolar air PO₂100 mmHg
Alveolar air PCO₂40 mmHg
Deoxygenated blood PO₂40 mmHg
Deoxygenated blood PCO₂46 mmHg
Oxygenated blood PO₂100 mmHg

💡 Why is Alveolar PO₂ Lower Than Atmospheric?

Three reasons: (1) Oxygen is constantly being absorbed into the blood, (2) Inhaled air is humidified, adding water vapor, and (3) Alveolar air mixes with residual air from previous breaths.

3The Respiratory Membrane & Gas Exchange

💡 The Big Idea

The respiratory membrane is extremely thin—only 0.2 μm—to allow rapid diffusion of gases. Gas exchange occurs by simple diffusion, not active transport.

🔑 External vs Internal Respiration

  • External Respiration: Gas exchange between alveoli and pulmonary capillaries (in the lungs)
  • Internal Respiration: Gas exchange between systemic capillaries and tissue cells (in the tissues)

Factors Affecting Gas Exchange Efficiency

Surface area↑ area = ↑ exchange
Membrane thickness↓ thickness = ↑ exchange
Partial pressure gradient↑ gradient = ↑ exchange
Blood flow↑ flow = ↑ exchange

🏥 Clinical Connection: Pulmonary Edema

Pulmonary edema (fluid in the lungs) DECREASES gas exchange efficiency by increasing the diffusion distance. The respiratory membrane becomes thicker with fluid, slowing gas diffusion.

4Oxygen Transport: Why Hemoglobin is Essential

💡 The Big Idea

Hemoglobin increases the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood by about 70-fold. Without hemoglobin, you couldn't survive.

Forms of O₂ Transport in Blood

Dissolved in plasma~1.5%
Bound to hemoglobin~98.5%

🔑 Hemoglobin Structure

Each hemoglobin molecule can bind a maximum of 4 oxygen molecules—one O₂ per heme group. When fully loaded, hemoglobin is "100% saturated."

Hb + O₂ ⇌ HbO₂
Deoxyhemoglobin + Oxygen ⇌ Oxyhemoglobin
This reaction is reversible—crucial for both loading AND unloading

The Oxygen-Hemoglobin Dissociation Curve

The curve is S-shaped (sigmoidal) due to cooperative binding—when one O₂ binds, it increases affinity for the next.

Hemoglobin Saturation at Different PO₂

PO₂ = 100 mmHg (arterial blood)~98% saturated
PO₂ = 40 mmHg (venous blood at rest)~75% saturated
PO₂ = 26 mmHg~50% saturated (P₅₀)

🔑 Why the S-Shape Matters

  • Flat upper portion (80-100 mmHg): Hb stays nearly saturated even if PO₂ drops—safety margin for O₂ loading in lungs
  • Steep middle portion (20-40 mmHg): Small drops in PO₂ cause large O₂ release—efficient unloading in tissues

Factors That Shift the O₂-Hb Curve

🔑 The Bohr Effect

↑CO₂, ↑temperature, ↓pH, ↑2,3-DPG shift the curve RIGHT → decreased Hb affinity → more O₂ released to tissues

Active tissues (hot, acidic, high CO₂) get MORE oxygen exactly when they need it!

🔑 2,3-DPG (2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate)

2,3-DPG is produced by red blood cells. It binds to deoxyhemoglobin and decreases oxygen affinity, shifting the curve RIGHT. Levels increase during chronic hypoxia (high altitude, anemia) to help tissues get more O₂.

🏥 Clinical Connection: Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

CO binds to hemoglobin with 200× greater affinity than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb). Two deadly effects:

  • CO occupies O₂ binding sites → reduced O₂ carrying capacity
  • CO shifts the O₂-Hb curve LEFT → remaining O₂ is held tighter and harder to unload to tissues

Treatment: 100% O₂ or hyperbaric O₂ to displace CO from hemoglobin.

5Carbon Dioxide Transport

Forms of CO₂ Transport in Blood

Dissolved in plasma5-6%
Bound to hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin)5-8%
As bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻)86-90%

🔑 Where Does CO₂ Bind on Hemoglobin?

CO₂ binds to the globin protein (amino acids), NOT the heme group. This is different from O₂, which binds to the iron in the heme. This means CO₂ and O₂ don't compete for the same binding sites—Hb can carry both simultaneously.

Bicarbonate Formation: The Main Transport Method

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺
Carbon dioxide + Water ⇌ Carbonic acid ⇌ Bicarbonate + Hydrogen ion
Catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase (CA) in RBCs

This reaction occurs primarily inside red blood cells where carbonic anhydrase is located. The enzyme speeds up the reaction ~5000×.

🔑 The Chloride Shift

After HCO₃⁻ is formed inside RBCs, it must leave to be transported in plasma. As bicarbonate exits the RBC, chloride (Cl⁻) enters to maintain electrical balance. This is the "chloride shift" (also called the Hamburger shift).

In the lungs, the process reverses: Cl⁻ exits, HCO₃⁻ enters, and CO₂ is reformed and exhaled.

🔑 The Haldane Effect

Deoxygenated hemoglobin binds CO₂ better than oxygenated hemoglobin.

  • In tissues: O₂ leaves Hb → Hb can now pick up more CO₂
  • In lungs: O₂ binds to Hb → CO₂ is released

This complements the Bohr effect: Bohr = CO₂ affects O₂ binding; Haldane = O₂ affects CO₂ binding.

6Control of Breathing

💡 The Big Idea

Breathing is controlled by respiratory centers in the brainstem (medulla and pons). The primary stimulus for breathing is CO₂ (via pH), not oxygen!

Respiratory Control Centers

Brainstem Respiratory Groups

Ventral Respiratory Group (VRG)Sets basic breathing rhythm
Dorsal Respiratory Group (DRG)Integrates sensory info, modifies breathing
Pontine Respiratory Group (PRG)Smooths transitions between inspiration/expiration

🔑 The Three Respiratory Centers

  • VRG (Medulla): Contains neurons that establish the basic rhythm of breathing—the "pacemaker" of respiration
  • DRG (Medulla): Receives input from chemoreceptors and lung stretch receptors; modifies breathing based on body's needs
  • PRG (Pons): Fine-tunes breathing patterns; helps create smooth transitions between inhalation and exhalation

Chemoreceptors: Detecting Blood Gas Levels

Central Chemoreceptors Peripheral Chemoreceptors
Location: Medulla oblongata (brainstem) Location: Carotid bodies and aortic bodies
Detect: pH of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Detect: Blood pH, PO₂, and PCO₂
Respond to O₂: NO Respond to O₂: Yes (when PO₂ < 60 mmHg)

🏥 Why CO₂ Matters More Than O₂

CO₂ directly affects blood pH through the bicarbonate equation. Even small changes in PCO₂ cause pH changes that can disrupt enzyme function. Your body is exquisitely sensitive to CO₂ because maintaining pH is critical for survival.

Effects of Hyper- and Hypoventilation

Blood Gas Changes

Hyperventilation↓CO₂, ↑pH (respiratory alkalosis)
Hypoventilation↑CO₂, ↓pH (respiratory acidosis)

🔑 Why Hyperventilation Makes You Dizzy

Rapid breathing blows off too much CO₂ → blood becomes alkaline → cerebral vasoconstriction → reduced blood flow to brain → dizziness, lightheadedness, tingling.

🔑 You Cannot Hold Your Breath Until You Die

Even with maximum willpower, rising CO₂ and falling pH will eventually override voluntary control and force you to breathe. The brainstem's respiratory centers are stronger than your conscious control. This is a survival mechanism—your body protects itself from suffocation.

7Ventilation-Perfusion Matching

💡 The Big Idea

Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) matching ensures blood flow is directed to well-ventilated alveoli. This optimizes gas exchange by matching airflow with blood flow.

🔑 Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction

When alveolar PO₂ is low (poorly ventilated area), local pulmonary arterioles CONSTRICT. This redirects blood away from poorly ventilated areas to better-ventilated regions.

This is UNIQUE to pulmonary circulation! In systemic circulation, low O₂ causes vasoDILATION (to bring more blood). In the lungs, you want blood to go where the O₂ IS.

V/Q Mismatch Conditions

Dead Space vs Shunt

Dead SpaceVentilation WITHOUT perfusion
ShuntPerfusion WITHOUT ventilation

🏥 Clinical Connection: Pulmonary Embolism vs Pneumonia

  • Pulmonary embolism (blood clot in pulmonary artery) → creates dead space: air reaches alveoli but no blood flows past to pick up O₂
  • Pneumonia (fluid-filled alveoli) → creates shunt: blood flows past alveoli but no air can reach them for gas exchange

Both conditions impair gas exchange but through opposite mechanisms.

1 Explain how partial pressure gradients drive gas exchange in both external and internal respiration. +
Answer:

Gases diffuse from high to low partial pressure.

In external respiration (lungs):

  • Alveolar PO₂ (100 mmHg) > Blood PO₂ (40 mmHg) → O₂ diffuses INTO blood
  • Blood PCO₂ (46 mmHg) > Alveolar PCO₂ (40 mmHg) → CO₂ diffuses OUT of blood

In internal respiration (tissues):

  • Blood PO₂ (100 mmHg) > Tissue PO₂ (≤40 mmHg) → O₂ diffuses into tissues
  • Tissue PCO₂ (≥46 mmHg) > Blood PCO₂ (40 mmHg) → CO₂ diffuses into blood

The steeper the gradient, the faster diffusion occurs—which is why active tissues (with very low PO₂) receive more oxygen.

2 Describe the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve. Why is its S-shape physiologically important? +
Answer:

The O₂-Hb dissociation curve plots hemoglobin saturation (%) against PO₂ (mmHg). Its S-shape (sigmoidal) has two critical regions:

  1. Flat upper portion (80-100 mmHg): Hb stays nearly saturated even if PO₂ drops—provides a safety margin for O₂ loading in lungs
  2. Steep middle portion (20-40 mmHg): Small drops in PO₂ cause large O₂ release—ensures efficient unloading in tissues

The S-shape results from cooperative binding: when one O₂ binds to Hb, it increases affinity for the next O₂. At tissues (low PO₂), releasing one O₂ decreases affinity, promoting further release.

3 Explain the Bohr effect and its physiological significance during exercise. +
Answer:

The Bohr effect describes how ↑CO₂, ↑temperature, ↓pH, and ↑2,3-DPG shift the O₂-Hb dissociation curve to the RIGHT, decreasing Hb's affinity for O₂ and promoting O₂ release.

During exercise: Active muscles produce more CO₂ and heat, and become more acidic (lactic acid)—all factors that shift the curve right.

This is physiologically brilliant: the tissues that need the most O₂ are precisely the ones creating conditions that make Hb release more O₂. It's an automatic matching of O₂ delivery to metabolic demand.

At the lungs (low CO₂, normal pH, cooler), the curve shifts back left, promoting O₂ loading.

4 Describe the three forms of CO₂ transport in blood. Which is most important and why? +
Answer:

CO₂ is transported in three forms:

  1. Dissolved in plasma (5-6%): CO₂ is more soluble than O₂ but still a minor fraction
  2. Bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin (5-8%): CO₂ binds to amino groups on Hb (not the heme iron)
  3. As bicarbonate ions HCO₃⁻ (86-90%): Most important form

In RBCs, carbonic anhydrase catalyzes: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → HCO₃⁻ + H⁺

The HCO₃⁻ exits via chloride shift; H⁺ is buffered by hemoglobin. This system is crucial because it carries most CO₂, helps buffer blood pH, and is reversible—in the lungs, the reaction reverses and CO₂ is exhaled.

5 Compare and contrast the Bohr effect and the Haldane effect. +
Answer:

Both effects describe how O₂ and CO₂ transport are coordinated:

Bohr effect: CO₂/H⁺ affect O₂ binding

  • ↑CO₂/↑H⁺ → ↓O₂ affinity → more O₂ released (important in tissues)

Haldane effect: O₂ affects CO₂ binding

  • ↓O₂ → ↑CO₂ binding capacity (deoxygenated Hb carries more CO₂)

Together they create elegant coordination:

  • In tissues: O₂ leaves Hb (Haldane: now Hb picks up more CO₂) while CO₂/H⁺ promote O₂ release (Bohr)
  • In lungs: O₂ binds Hb (Haldane: CO₂ is released) while low CO₂ promotes O₂ binding (Bohr)
6 Why is CO₂, not O₂, the primary stimulus for breathing? What role do chemoreceptors play? +
Answer:

CO₂ is the primary stimulus because it directly affects blood pH through the bicarbonate equation (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺). Even small PCO₂ changes alter pH, which can disrupt enzyme function.

Central chemoreceptors in the medulla detect pH changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CO₂ crosses the blood-brain barrier, converts to H⁺).

Peripheral chemoreceptors in carotid bodies detect blood PCO₂, pH, and PO₂.

When PCO₂ rises (or pH falls), chemoreceptors stimulate increased ventilation to blow off CO₂.

O₂ only becomes a significant stimulus when PO₂ drops below ~60 mmHg—a dangerous level. This is a backup system for emergencies.

7 Explain ventilation-perfusion matching and why hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is unique. +
Answer:

Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) matching ensures blood flow goes where the air goes, optimizing gas exchange.

If an area has poor ventilation (low alveolar PO₂), local pulmonary arterioles CONSTRICT, redirecting blood to better-ventilated areas.

This "hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction" is unique to pulmonary circulation—in systemic circulation, low O₂ causes vasoDILATION (to bring more blood/O₂).

The difference makes sense: in systemic tissues, you want MORE blood when O₂ is low; in lungs, you want blood to go where O₂ IS available.

8 Explain why carbon monoxide poisoning is so dangerous, even at low concentrations. +
Answer:

Carbon monoxide (CO) is extremely dangerous for two reasons:

  1. CO binds hemoglobin with 200-250× greater affinity than O₂, so even tiny amounts occupy Hb binding sites, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb)
  2. CO shifts the O₂-Hb dissociation curve LEFT, increasing Hb's affinity for remaining O₂—tissues can't extract what IS being carried

Result: tissues starve for O₂ even though blood appears well-oxygenated (cherry-red skin color).

Because CO is colorless and odorless, and pulse oximetry can't distinguish COHb from O₂Hb, poisoning can go undetected.

Treatment: 100% O₂ or hyperbaric O₂ to displace CO from Hb.

9 What force(s) move(s) carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveoli? +
Answer:

Carbon dioxide moves into the alveoli as a result of the pressure gradient.

Blood arriving at the pulmonary capillaries has PCO₂ of ~46 mmHg, while alveolar air has PCO₂ of ~40 mmHg. This gradient drives CO₂ diffusion from blood into alveoli.

The process reverses the reactions that occurred in tissues: HCO₃⁻ re-enters RBCs, combines with H⁺ to form carbonic acid, which carbonic anhydrase converts back to CO₂ and H₂O. The CO₂ then diffuses across the respiratory membrane.

10 List and explain the factors that influence gas diffusion in the lungs. +
Answer:

Four factors affect gas diffusion rate:

  1. Concentration gradient (partial pressure difference): Greater gradient = faster diffusion
  2. Surface area: Increased surface area = increased diffusion. The lungs have ~70 m² of surface area
  3. Membrane thickness: Increased thickness = decreased diffusion. The respiratory membrane is only 0.2 μm thick
  4. Diffusion distance: Longer distance = slower diffusion

Increasing the concentration gradient and surface area will increase diffusion rate, while increasing membrane thickness and diffusion distance will decrease diffusion rate.

11 Name three pathological changes that adversely affect gas exchange. +
Answer:

Three conditions that impair gas exchange:

  1. Decreased alveolar surface area (emphysema): Destruction of alveolar walls reduces area for gas exchange
  2. Increased alveolar membrane thickness (pulmonary fibrosis): Scar tissue thickens the respiratory membrane
  3. Increased diffusion distance (pulmonary edema): Fluid accumulation between alveoli and capillaries increases the path gases must travel
12 List and explain the factors that influence the presence of gases in liquids. +
Answer:

According to Henry's Law, three factors affect gas dissolution in liquids:

  1. Partial pressure: Higher partial pressure = more gas dissolves
  2. Solubility: Some gases dissolve more readily (CO₂ is ~24× more soluble than O₂ in water)
  3. Temperature: Lower temperature = more gas dissolves (gases are less soluble at higher temperatures)
13 What are the three ways CO₂ is transported in blood? Approximately what percentage is transported by each way? +
Answer:
  1. Attached to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin: ~23% (binds to amino groups on the globin protein, NOT the heme)
  2. Dissolved in plasma: ~7%
  3. As bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) in plasma: ~70% (most important form)

The bicarbonate transport is catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase in RBCs: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺

14 List and describe the brain's centers for monitoring and controlling respiration. +
Answer:

Respiratory neurons in the medulla control inspiration and expiration. Neurons in the pons modulate ventilation.

  • Ventral Respiratory Group (VRG): Located in the medulla; contains the rhythm-generating neurons that establish the basic breathing pattern
  • Dorsal Respiratory Group (DRG): Located in the nucleus tractus solitarius of the medulla; receives sensory input from peripheral chemoreceptors and stretch receptors; modulates breathing based on these inputs
  • Pontine Respiratory Group (PRG): Located in the pons; helps smooth transitions between inspiration and expiration; fine-tunes breathing patterns

Ventilation is subject to modulation by various chemical factors and by higher brain centers.

15 List and describe the locations and stimuli for respiratory chemoreceptors. +
Answer:

Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies sense changes in:

  • Oxygen concentration (PO₂)
  • pH
  • PCO₂ of the plasma

Central chemoreceptors in the medulla oblongata monitor cerebrospinal fluid composition and respond to changes in:

  • Concentration of CO₂ in the cerebrospinal fluid (detected via pH changes)

Note: Central chemoreceptors do NOT directly respond to O₂ levels.

16 Which is usually more important in regulating the respiratory system, PO₂ or PCO₂? Explain your answer and discuss the receptors involved. +
Answer:

PCO₂ is the more important factor.

For PCO₂, there are central and peripheral receptors that respond to CO₂ as well as to CO₂-related pH. These receptors are very sensitive to routine changes in PCO₂ and pH, such as those associated with an increase in physical activity.

Peripheral chemoreceptors have been identified for O₂, but these respond only to dramatic changes in PO₂ (below ~60 mmHg), such as those associated with high altitude or disease.

Because CO₂ is produced as a by-product of aerobic (oxygen-consuming) metabolism, an increase in CO₂ is associated with a corresponding decrease in O₂.

The sensitivity to CO₂ makes physiological sense because CO₂ directly affects blood pH, which can disrupt enzyme function and cellular processes if not tightly controlled.

17 A 10-year-old swimmer has attached an extension to his snorkel tube, so that he can sit on the bottom of the ocean for a longer period of time. What problems would you expect him to have? +
Answer:

The resistance to air flow increases as a result of adding the extension to the snorkel tube. Assuming he has used an extension of sufficiently large diameter, he is in a shallow location where the total length isn't excessive, and he has the strength and endurance in his respiratory muscles, he may be able to breathe this way for a few seconds longer than he could have held his breath.

He will probably notice that he is breathing more deeply and at a higher rate, to maintain ventilation against the higher resistance. After a few exchanges he will start to hypoventilate, despite his efforts to increase pulmonary ventilation, because the presence of dead airspace in the tube will result in decreasing PO₂ in the alveoli.

Dead airspace is a problem if the total volume of air in the tube is greater than the volume exchanged with each breath.

18 Cary deliberately hyperventilates for several minutes before diving into a swimming pool. Shortly after he enters the water and begins swimming, he blacks out and almost drowns. What caused this to happen? +
Answer:

Hyperventilation causes a decrease in the alveolar PCO₂, and more carbon dioxide is eliminated from the body than during normal breathing. The loss of large amounts of carbon dioxide upsets the body's normal drive for ventilation, and Cary does not feel the urge to breathe as he swims.

As the exercising muscles use oxygen, a state of hypoxia develops. This results in insufficient amounts of oxygen reaching the brain, causing Cary to lose consciousness.

This is dangerous because:

  • The O₂ stores are depleted before CO₂ builds up enough to trigger the urge to breathe
  • PO₂ can drop below 60 mmHg (the threshold for peripheral chemoreceptor stimulation) without warning
  • Hypoxia causes rapid loss of consciousness underwater
19 Define hyperventilation and explain what may cause it. How are PO₂ and PCO₂ affected by hyperventilation? Can breathing into a paper bag decrease hyperventilation? Explain. +
Answer:

Hyperventilation is an increase in alveolar ventilation that exceeds metabolic demand. A person can deliberately hyperventilate, or it may occur as a result of emotional stress or high altitude.

Strictly speaking, the increase in ventilation during exercise is necessary to meet increased metabolism and is therefore not hyperventilation.

Effects on blood gases:

  • PO₂ is increased — more O₂ is inhaled
  • PCO₂ is decreased — CO₂ is exhaled faster than it's produced, producing abnormally low PCO₂

Paper bag treatment: Hyperventilation can be remedied by breathing into a paper bag, because rebreathing exhaled air will increase PCO₂ in the body back to normal. The elevated CO₂ restores normal blood pH and stops the symptoms (dizziness, tingling, lightheadedness).

20 Do the factors affecting affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen have the same effect on loading and unloading oxygen in lungs and tissues? Explain your answer. +
Answer:

Hemoglobin affinity changes in the same way in the lungs and tissues. Factors that decrease affinity (↑CO₂, ↑temperature, ↓pH, ↑2,3-BPG) shift the curve RIGHT everywhere.

At first glance, this seems to present a conflict: if the curve shifts right, wouldn't that decrease loading in the lungs while increasing unloading in tissues?

The key insight is that the effect is more pronounced in tissues due to the steep portion of the dissociation curve:

  • In the lungs (PO₂ ~100 mmHg): The curve is flat at the top, so affinity changes have minimal effect—Hb still gets nearly saturated
  • In the tissues (PO₂ ~40 mmHg or lower): The curve is steep, so the same affinity decrease causes significant additional O₂ release

The net effect is that increased unloading in tissues (where it's needed) outweighs any small decrease in loading in the lungs.
