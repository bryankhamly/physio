Chapter 23

The Immune System

Defense, recognition, and memory

1

Overview

Two Types of Immunity

  • Innate immunity: Nonspecific, present at birth, immediate response
  • Adaptive immunity: Specific, acquired, develops after exposure
First Line of Defense
Skin & mucous membranes
Lymphocyte Population
~1 trillion in adults

Key Distinction

  • Innate: Same response every time (no memory)
  • Adaptive: Faster & stronger on re-exposure (memory cells)

Four Basic Steps of Immune Response

  1. Detection: Recognize foreign invader
  2. Communication: Alert other immune cells
  3. Recruitment: Mobilize defenses to site
  4. Elimination: Destroy pathogen
2

Physical & Chemical Barriers

Physical Barriers

  • Skin: Largest organ, first line of defense
    • Epidermis: Outer layer with keratin (waterproof)
    • Dermis: Inner layer with blood vessels, nerves
    • Sebaceous glands: Secrete fatty acids (antimicrobial)
  • Mucous membranes: Viscous mucus traps foreign matter
  • Ciliated epithelium: Cilia sweep trapped particles up and out
  • GI tract mucosa: Physical barrier + acid + enzymes

Chemical Barriers

  • Lysozyme: In saliva and tears - breaks down bacterial cell walls
  • HCl in stomach: Kills most ingested pathogens (pH ~2)
  • Acid in urinary tract: Prevents bacterial growth
  • Acid in vagina: Maintains low pH to prevent infection
  • Fatty acids from sebaceous glands: Antimicrobial activity on skin

Key Point

Physical and chemical barriers are part of innate immunity - they're present at birth and provide nonspecific protection against ALL pathogens.

Pathogens

Four Classes of Pathogens

Virulence = ability to cause disease

TypeSizeCharacteristics
Viruses20-400 nm (smallest)Nucleic acid + protein coat, require host, can double hourly, target specific cells
Bacteria1-5 μmProkaryotic, single circular chromosome, Gram +/-, release endotoxins/exotoxins
FungiVariableEukaryotic, absorb nutrients, grow slowly (yeast, molds, mushrooms)
Parasites10 μm - 20 cmProtozoans (sleeping sickness) or Metazoans (worms, arthropods)

Virus Characteristics

  • Single- or double-stranded nucleic acid surrounded by protein coat (capsid)
  • Must use host's metabolic machinery to reproduce
  • Can double number of viral organisms hourly
  • Target specific cell types
  • NOT killed by antibiotics

Bacteria Characteristics

  • Gram-positive or Gram-negative: Based on cell wall structure
  • Adhesin: Proteins that allow colonization of tissues
  • Endotoxins: Released when bacteria die (cell wall component)
  • Exotoxins: Actively secreted by living bacteria

Antibiotics Target

Bacteria ONLY - NOT viruses, fungi, or parasites!

Parasites & Vectors

Vector: An organism that transmits parasites (e.g., mosquitoes for malaria, tsetse flies for sleeping sickness)

  • Collections of B cells, T cells, and macrophages

Immune Cells

Hematopoietic Stem Cell Lineages

All immune cells derive from hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow:

Myeloid Stem Cell Lineage

Myeloid stem cell → Granulocytes (Neutrophil, Eosinophil, Basophil)
Myeloid stem cell → Monocyte → Macrophage / Dendritic cell
Myeloid stem cell → Megakaryocyte → Platelets | Reticulocyte → RBCs

Lymphoid Stem Cell Lineage

Lymphoid stem cell → B lymphocytes (mature in bone marrow)
Lymphoid stem cell → T lymphocytes (mature in thymus)
Lymphoid stem cell → Natural killer (NK) cells

Granulocytes vs Agranulocytes

  • Granulocytes: Neutrophils, Eosinophils, Basophils (have cytoplasmic granules)
  • Agranulocytes: Monocytes, Lymphocytes (no visible granules)

Lymphocytes - Three Types

  • B cells: Contact antigen → plasma cell → secrete antibodies (immunoglobulins)
  • T cells: Directly damage foreign cells, takes several days to develop into cytotoxic T cells
  • Null cells: Most are natural killer cells - fast acting, early immune response, attack virus-infected cells causing lysis

Phagocytes

Cells that perform phagocytosis:

  • Neutrophils (most abundant)
  • Eosinophils
  • Monocytes
  • Macrophages
  • Dendritic cells

Phagocytes circulate 7-10 hours in blood, then migrate to tissues. Numbers increase 4-5x during infections.

Tissue-Specific Macrophages

  • Microglia: Brain
  • Kupffer cells: Liver
  • Langerhans cells: Skin (dendritic cells)
  • Alveolar macrophages: Lungs

Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs)

Present antigens to T cells via MHC molecules:

  • Dendritic cells - most effective APCs
  • Macrophages
  • B cells

NOT APCs: Neutrophils, T cells

Cell Counts Indicate Infection Type

  • ↑ Neutrophils + Macrophages: Bacterial infection
  • ↑ Eosinophils + Monocytes: Parasitic infection
  • ↑ Lymphocytes, Eosinophils, Basophils: Allergic reaction
  • ↑ Basophils: Inflammation

Innate Immunity

Nonspecific Defenses (Innate Defenses)

  • Present at birth
  • Nonspecific - same response to all pathogens
  • No memory - same strength each time
  • Immediate response

Pattern Recognition

  • PAMPs: Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (on pathogens)
  • Pattern recognition receptors: Bind PAMPs to trigger response
  • Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are a type of PRR

Components of Innate Immunity

  • Physical barriers (skin, mucous membranes)
  • Inflammation
  • Interferons
  • Natural killer cells
  • Complement system

NK Cells - Early Defense

  • Part of innate immunity (null cells)
  • Recognize abnormal or infected cells
  • Cause lysis by secreting perforins
  • Can attack virus-infected cells without identifying the virus
  • Fast acting - early defense against viral infections
  • Actions enhanced by IL-2 (from T cells) and antibodies (from plasma cells)

Inflammation

What is Inflammation?

A series of events causing accumulation of proteins, fluid, and phagocytes in an injured or invaded area.

Five Major Events of Inflammation

  1. Macrophages engulf debris and foreign matter - Proteins on microbes bound by macrophages triggers phagocytosis and cytokine secretion
  2. Capillaries dilate and become more permeable - Histamine from mast cells causes vasodilation → redness, heat, edema, pain
  3. Foreign matter is contained - Heparin prevents initial clotting so cells can access area; clot eventually forms to prevent spread
  4. More leukocytes migrate to area - Neutrophils arrive in 1 hour; monocytes in 10 hours → become macrophages
  5. Leukocytes clear the infection - Phagocytosis and cytokine secretion eliminate pathogens

Cardinal Signs of Inflammation

All caused by histamine effects:

  • Redness - vasodilation increases blood flow
  • Heat - increased blood brings warmth
  • Swelling (Edema) - increased permeability allows fluid out
  • Pain - swelling and chemical signals stimulate nerves

Leukocyte Migration Steps

  1. Margination: Leukocytes move to the blood vessel wall
  2. Attachment: Bind to the endothelial wall
  3. Diapedesis: Squeeze between endothelial cells into tissue
  4. Chemotaxis: Move through tissue toward injury site (following chemical signals)
Neutrophil Arrival
~1 hour
Monocyte Arrival
~10 hours

Heparin's Role

  • Released by mast cells and basophils
  • Prevents clot formation initially so blood cells can access the area
  • Clotting factors eventually form a clot to contain foreign matter
  • Clot eventually forms a scab

Leukocyte Proliferation

  • Cytokines travel to bone marrow
  • Stimulate production of more leukocytes
  • Circulating numbers can increase 4-5 times

Steps of Phagocytosis

  1. Attachment: Phagocyte recognizes damaged cells or protein-targeted cells (opsonins = proteins that tag cells for phagocytosis)
  2. Internalization: Takes only ~0.01 seconds! Phagosome + lysosome → secondary lysosome
  3. Degradation: Lysosomal enzymes break down phagocytosed material
  4. Exocytosis: Elimination of degradation products

Opsonins

Opsonins are proteins that tag pathogens for phagocytosis. They "prepare food" for phagocytes (Greek origin). Examples include antibodies and complement proteins (C3b).

Macrophage Secretory Products

  • IL-1: Stimulates proliferation and differentiation of B and T lymphocytes
  • IL-6: Induces acute phase response, fever
  • TNF-α (Tumor Necrosis Factor): Inflammation, can kill tumor cells

Actions of IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α

  • Bone marrow: Produces and secretes more leukocytes
  • Act as endogenous pyrogens: Stimulate fever via hypothalamus
  • Hypothalamus: Increases body temperature via prostaglandins
  • Liver: Produces acute-phase proteins
  • IL-1 specifically: Stimulates B cells → antibody production; T cells → cell-mediated immunity

Endogenous Pyrogens

IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α act as endogenous pyrogens (body's own fever-inducing substances). They signal the hypothalamus to raise body temperature via prostaglandins.

Acute Phase Proteins

  • Produced by liver in response to IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α
  • C-reactive protein (CRP) is an acute phase protein
  • ↑ CRP indicates inflammation/infection

Interferons

Proteins secreted by leukocytes and virus-infected cells that induce virus resistance:

  • Interferon-α and Interferon-β:
    • Secreted by virus-infected cells
    • Kill host cells (sacrifice to stop spread)
    • Induce neighboring cells to resist viral infection
  • Interferon-γ:
    • Secreted from active T cells and NK cells
    • Inhibits viral replication
    • Enhances phagocytosis by macrophages
    • Boosts antibody production in B cells
    • Helps activate NK cells and cytotoxic T cells
    • Inhibits cell division - defense against cancer

Memory Trick for Chemical Signals

  • Chemotaxins = Attract cells (chemotaxis)
  • Opsonins = Tag for eating (Greek: "to prepare food")
  • Pyrogens = Fire/Fever
  • Interferons = Interfere with viruses

Complement System

Overview

  • Approximately 30 plasma proteins that lyse foreign cells (especially bacteria)
  • Part of both innate AND adaptive immunity
  • Cascade activation results in MAC reaching bacterial surface
  • MAC (Membrane Attack Complex) pierces bacterial membrane → lysis

Three Activation Pathways

  1. Classical Pathway: Binding to antibodies attached to bacteria (part of specific/adaptive immunity)
  2. Lectin Pathway: Binding to mannose-containing carbohydrates on bacteria or viruses
  3. Alternative Pathway: Binding to carbohydrates on bacterial cell walls (part of nonspecific/innate immunity)

All Pathways Converge

Classical/Lectin/Alternative → C3 convertase → C3a + C3b → C5-C9 → MAC

Complement Protein Functions

  • Form MAC: Lyse bacterial cells by creating pores
  • Chemotaxis: C5a attracts leukocytes to site
  • Histamine release: C3a, C4a, C5a trigger mast cell degranulation
  • Opsonization: C3b acts as an opsonin (tags for phagocytosis)

MAC Formation and Lysis

Complement binds bacteria → Cascade activation → Pore/MAC forms → Fluid rushes in → Swelling → Lysis
10

Adaptive Immunity

Features of Specific Immune Response

  • Specificity: Targets specific antigens only
  • Diversity: Can recognize millions of different antigens
  • Memory: Faster and stronger response on re-exposure
  • Self-tolerance: Does not attack normal body cells
Humoral Immunity
B cells → Plasma cells → Antibodies
Cell-Mediated Immunity
T cells → Direct killing (cytotoxic T cells)

Antigens & Epitopes

  • Antigens: Complex proteins/polysaccharides on foreign invaders and tumor cells
  • Epitopes: Recognition sites on antigens that B or T cells bind to
  • B cells: Use membrane antibodies as antigen receptors
  • T cells: Use T cell receptors (TCRs) to recognize antigens

Clonal Selection

Antigen contacts lymphocyte → Lymphocyte proliferates → Differentiates → Effector cells + Memory cells
  • Effector cells: Combat the antigen, short-lived
  • Memory cells: Long-lived, antigen-specific

Primary vs Secondary Response

  • Primary response: First exposure, takes 10-17 days (symptoms occur during this time)
  • Secondary response: Subsequent exposures, takes only 2-7 days, greater magnitude, more prolonged (due to memory cells)

Self-Tolerance

  • B and T cells do NOT attack normal body cells
  • As cells develop, any with antigen receptors against normal body cells are destroyed by apoptosis (programmed cell death)
  • Failure of self-tolerance → autoimmune diseases
TypeDefinitionMemory?Example
Active immunityBody makes own antibodies after exposureYesInfection, vaccination
Passive immunityReceive ready-made antibodies from another sourceNoBreast milk, antivenom, placental transfer

Vaccination (Immunization)

  • Vaccine: Introduction of microorganism or its antigens in a form NOT expected to cause disease
  • Induces immune response including memory cell production
  • This is active immunity (your body does the work)

Passive Immunity from Mother

  • IgG passes to fetus via the placenta
  • IgA passes to baby in breast milk
  • No memory cells transferred, so no long-term immunity

Antibodies (Immunoglobulins)

B Lymphocytes in Antibody Production

  • B cell exposure to antigen triggers clonal selection
  • Produces memory B cells and plasma cells
  • Plasma cell life span: 4-7 days
  • Plasma cells secrete 2000 antibodies per second specific for antigen
  • Antibodies circulate for several weeks, binding and marking antigens

T-Dependent vs T-Independent Antigens

  • T-dependent antigens:
    • Need helper T cells for full B cell activation
    • Helper T cells secrete IL-2 which activates B cells
    • Produces both plasma cells AND memory cells
    • Stronger response
  • T-independent antigens:
    • Polysaccharides on bacterial surfaces
    • B cells can differentiate into plasma cells WITHOUT T cell help
    • Weaker response, NO memory cells
    • No lasting immunity for subsequent exposures

Antibody Structure

  • 2 Heavy chains + 2 Light chains
  • Variable (V) region: Antigen-binding site - differs for different antigens, gives specificity
  • Constant (C) region: Same within a class of antibodies - determines function
  • Two antigen-binding sites per antibody
  • Connected by disulfide bridges

Who Makes Antibodies?

Plasma cells (differentiated B cells) - NOT T cells, NOT B cells themselves!

Immunoglobulin Memory Tricks

  • IgG = "G"oes through placenta, "G"reatest amount
  • IgA = "A"ll secretions (mucosal surfaces, breast milk)
  • IgM = "M"ade first (pri"M"ary response)
  • IgE = all"E"rgies
  • IgD = on B cell surface (receptor)

Antibody Actions

  • All classes: Neutralization and agglutination
  • IgM and IgG: Activate complement (classical pathway)
  • IgG: Opsonization (tags for phagocytosis)
  • IgE: Triggers histamine release from mast cells and basophils
  • IgG + NK cells: NK cells have receptors for IgG tail - antibodies mark cells for destruction by NK cells

High IgM Level Indicates

Person is currently fighting an infection (first antibody made). High IgG means past infection or vaccination.

T Cells & MHC

T Cell Types

  • Helper T cells (CD4+): Secrete cytokines that enhance activity of B cells, other T cells, macrophages, and NK cells
  • Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+): Kill virus-infected cells, abnormal cells, and bacteria
  • Suppressor T cells: Secrete cytokines that suppress activity of B cells and other T cells

MHC Molecules

  • T cells can ONLY recognize antigen when associated with MHC molecules
  • MHC molecules bind antigen inside the cell and transport it to the surface (antigen presentation)
  • MHC marks body cells as "self"
MHC ClassFound OnPresents ToFunction
Class I MHCAll nucleated cellsCytotoxic T cells (CD8+)Presents antigens from inside the cell (viral proteins, tumor antigens)
Class II MHCAPCs only (macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells, activated T cells)Helper T cells (CD4+)Presents antigens taken up by phagocytosis/endocytosis

HLA - Human Leukocyte Antigen

  • HLA = MHC molecules unique to each individual
  • Determines tissue type
  • Marks person as "self"
  • Responsible for tissue/organ rejection - stimulates immune response to foreign tissue

T Cell Maturation Depends on MHC Contact

  • Bind with Class I MHC → become Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+)
  • Bind with Class II MHC → become Helper T cells (CD4+)

Helper T Cell Activation

  1. Helper T cell binds to Class II MHC-antigen complex on macrophages/B cells
  2. Macrophages/B cells secrete IL-1 → induces T cell proliferation
  3. Helper T cells differentiate into secretory (effector) cells and memory cells
  4. Secretory cells secrete cytokines that regulate immune response
CytokineTargetsEffects
IL-2T cells, B cells, NK cellsProliferation of helper T & cytotoxic T cells; B cell proliferation & plasma cell development; Enhances NK activity
IL-4B cells, T cells, Macrophages, Mast cellsB cell proliferation, IgE/IgG production; T cell proliferation; Class II MHC expression
IL-5B cells, Bone marrowB cell proliferation, IgA production; Eosinophil production

Cytotoxic T Cell Activation & Killing

  1. Cytotoxic T cell binds to Class I MHC-antigen on infected/tumor cell
  2. Helper T cells secrete IL-2 which activates the cytotoxic T cell
  3. Cytotoxic T cell secretes perforins → form pores in target cell membrane → lysis
  4. Cytotoxic T cell secretes fragmentins → enter through pores → trigger apoptosis

Perforins vs Fragmentins

  • Perforins: Form pores in cell membrane → causes lysis (cell bursts)
  • Fragmentins: Enter through pores and trigger apoptosis (programmed cell death)
  • Both are used by cytotoxic T cells AND NK cells

Cancer Cells and MHC

  • Tumor cells have tumor antigens
  • Class I MHC presents these antigens to cytotoxic T cells
  • Some cancers and viruses inhibit Class I MHC production → protects them from immune system!

Helper T Cells are Central

A decrease in helper T cells would decrease ALL parts of immune response (they coordinate everything). This is why HIV/AIDS is so devastating - it destroys CD4+ helper T cells.

Clinical Applications

Blood Types & Compatibility

  • Type A: A antigens on RBCs, anti-B antibodies in plasma
  • Type B: B antigens on RBCs, anti-A antibodies in plasma
  • Type AB: Both antigens, NO antibodies (universal recipient)
  • Type O: No antigens, both antibodies (universal donor)

Tissue Grafts & Organ Transplantation

  • HLA molecules (MHC) stimulate rejection by inducing immune response
  • Tissue typing: Matches HLA molecules between donor and recipient
  • Must suppress recipient's immune response
  • Cyclosporin A and FK506: Inhibit IL-2 to prevent rejection

Bone Marrow Transplants

  • Radiation treatment destroys recipient's bone marrow (inactivates immune system)
  • Donated marrow may mount immune response against recipient
  • Graft versus host reaction: Donor immune cells attack recipient
  • Critical to match donor and recipient HLA molecules

Allergy (Hypersensitivity)

  • Allergens: Antigens that induce allergic reactions
  • IgE is the antibody of allergies
  • IgE activates mast cells to release histamine
  • Symptoms: sneezing, coughing, runny nose, itching

Anaphylactic Shock

  • Severe allergic reaction
  • Massive release of histamine from mast cells throughout body
  • Vasodilation → ↓ TPR → ↓ MAP (dangerously low blood pressure)
  • Treatment: Epinephrine - increases CO and TPR → increases MAP

Autoimmune Diseases

Immune system treats part of self like a pathogen:

  • Type 1 Diabetes (IDDM): Attacks pancreatic beta cells
  • Lupus: Attacks multiple organs/tissues
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: Attacks joint tissues
  • Multiple sclerosis: Attacks myelin sheath of neurons

Immunodeficiency Diseases

  • SCID (Severe Combined Immunodeficiency): Affects BOTH humoral and cell-mediated immunity ("bubble boy" disease)
  • Hodgkin's disease: Cancer of lymphatic system
  • AIDS: HIV destroys helper T cells (CD4+), devastating because helper T cells coordinate entire immune response

Stress and Immunity

  • Stress suppresses the immune system through multiple mechanisms:
  • Cortisol (steroid hormone):
    • Decreases number of leukocytes
    • Has anti-inflammatory activity
  • Environmental chemicals can affect immunity
  • Autonomic neural input to lymphoid tissue
  • Multiple levels of interaction between stress and immune system

Summary: Immune Dysfunctions

TypeProblemExample
AllergyOveractive response to harmless antigenHay fever, food allergies

Key Takeaways

  • Innate = nonspecific, no memory; Adaptive = specific, diversity, memory, self-tolerance
  • Chemical barriers: lysozyme (saliva), HCl (stomach), fatty acids (skin), acid (urinary/vagina)
  • Myeloid stem cells → granulocytes, monocytes; Lymphoid stem cells → B cells, T cells, NK cells
  • Phagocytes circulate 7-10 hours in blood; numbers increase 4-5x during infection
  • Inflammation: 5 major events; leukocyte migration = margination → attachment → diapedesis → chemotaxis
  • Phagocytosis: attachment → internalization (0.01 sec!) → degradation → exocytosis
  • Macrophage cytokines: IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α act as endogenous pyrogens via hypothalamus/prostaglandins
  • Interferon-γ (from T cells/NK cells) inhibits viral replication, enhances phagocytosis, inhibits cancer
  • Complement: 3 pathways (classical, lectin, alternative) → all converge → MAC → lysis
  • Primary response: 10-17 days; Secondary response: 2-7 days (memory cells)
  • Plasma cells: lifespan 4-7 days, secrete 2000 antibodies/second
  • IgG = most abundant, crosses placenta; IgM = first made, activates complement; IgE = allergies; IgA = secretions
  • T-dependent antigens: need helper T cells, produce memory; T-independent: weaker, no memory
  • MHC I (all nucleated cells) → CD8+ cytotoxic T cells; MHC II (APCs only) → CD4+ helper T cells
  • HLA = unique MHC; determines tissue type; causes organ rejection
  • Cytotoxic T cells use perforins (pores → lysis) and fragmentins (apoptosis)
  • Anaphylactic shock: massive histamine → ↓TPR → ↓MAP; treat with epinephrine
  • Stress → cortisol → immunosuppression (decreased leukocytes, anti-inflammatory)
