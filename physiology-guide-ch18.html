Chapter 18 • Urinary System

Renal Function

How your kidneys filter blood and make urine

🎯 What You'll Learn

  • Why kidneys are essential for survival (it's not just about making urine)
  • The anatomy of the nephron—the kidney's functional unit
  • The three basic processes: filtration, reabsorption, and secretion
  • How your body regulates filtration rate to maintain homeostasis
  • What each part of the tubule does and why it matters

1The Big Picture: What Do Kidneys Actually Do?

💡 The Big Idea

Kidneys regulate the composition, volume, and pressure of your blood. They filter about 180 liters of fluid daily, selectively keeping what you need and excreting what you don't.

The Many Jobs of Your Kidneys

  • Excrete metabolic wastes — Urea, creatinine, uric acid
  • Regulate water balance — Control how much water you retain or excrete
  • Regulate electrolyte balance — Control levels of Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻
  • Regulate blood pH — Excrete or retain H⁺ and HCO₃⁻
  • Regulate blood pressure — Via fluid volume and RAAS
  • Produce hormones — Erythropoietin (RBC production), calcitriol (vitamin D)

Note: Kidneys do NOT produce red blood cells—they produce erythropoietin, which stimulates the bone marrow to make RBCs!

🔑 Kidney Hormones

  • Erythropoietin (EPO): Produced in response to hypoxia; stimulates RBC production in bone marrow
  • Calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D₃): The active form of vitamin D—kidneys perform the final activation step
  • Renin: An enzyme that initiates the RAAS cascade

💭 Think of kidneys like a water treatment plant...

A city's water treatment facility processes millions of gallons daily. It doesn't just remove waste—it carefully monitors and adjusts mineral content, pH, and volume. Your kidneys do the same for blood, processing it ~40 times per day!

2Kidney Anatomy

🔑 Gross Anatomy

  • Renal cortex: Outer region—contains all glomeruli and parts of tubules
  • Renal medulla: Inner region—contains renal pyramids with loops of Henle and collecting ducts
  • Renal pelvis: Funnel-shaped structure that collects urine from collecting ducts → drains to ureter
  • Renal columns: Extensions of cortical tissue between pyramids

The Nephron: The Functional Unit

Each kidney contains approximately 1 million nephrons. The nephron consists of:

🔑 Nephron Components

  • Renal corpuscle: = Glomerulus + Bowman's capsule (where filtration occurs)
  • Glomerulus: A ball of capillaries where blood is filtered
  • Bowman's capsule: Cup-like structure surrounding glomerulus that collects filtrate
  • Renal tubule: PCT → Loop of Henle → DCT → Collecting duct

Blood Flow Through the Nephron

Blood enters viaAfferent arteriole
Blood is filtered inGlomerulus
Blood exits viaEfferent arteriole
Blood then flows throughPeritubular capillaries

🔑 Two Capillary Beds in Series

The nephron is unique—it has two capillary beds connected by an arteriole (not a venule):

  • Glomerular capillaries: HIGH pressure (~60 mmHg) → favors FILTRATION
  • Peritubular capillaries: LOW pressure (~10 mmHg) + high oncotic pressure → favors REABSORPTION

This arrangement allows precise control of both filtration and reabsorption.

🔑 Cortical vs Juxtamedullary Nephrons

  • Cortical nephrons (80-85%): Short loops of Henle; glomeruli in outer cortex
  • Juxtamedullary nephrons (15-20%): Long loops extending deep into medulla; essential for creating the medullary osmotic gradient and concentrating urine

🔑 The Juxtaglomerular Apparatus (JGA)

Located where the DCT contacts the afferent arteriole near its glomerulus. Contains:

  • Macula densa cells: In the DCT wall—sense NaCl concentration in tubular fluid
  • Granular (JG) cells: In the afferent arteriole wall—secrete renin
  • The JGA is critical for autoregulation of GFR and initiation of RAAS

3The Three Basic Renal Processes

💡 The Big Idea

The kidney uses a "filter first, then reclaim" strategy. It filters almost EVERYTHING out of blood (except cells and proteins), then selectively reabsorbs what the body needs.

🔑 The Three Processes

  • Filtration (Blood → Bowman's Capsule): Pressure forces water and small solutes through the filtration membrane
  • Reabsorption (Tubule → Blood): Needed substances transported back into peritubular capillaries
  • Secretion (Blood → Tubule): Some substances actively transported FROM blood INTO the tubule
Amount Excreted = Amount Filtered + Amount Secreted − Amount Reabsorbed
This equation determines how much of any substance ends up in urine

4Glomerular Filtration

🔑 The Filtration Membrane

The filtration membrane consists of three layers:

  1. Fenestrated endothelium: Capillary cells with small pores
  2. Basement membrane: Protein-polysaccharide gel that blocks large molecules
  3. Podocytes: Specialized epithelial cells with filtration slits

What gets filtered: Water, glucose, ions, amino acids, small molecules

What stays in blood: Red blood cells, plasma proteins (too large and negatively charged)

GFR: The Numbers

Normal GFR125 mL/min
Daily filtrate volume~180 liters!
Daily urine output~1.5 liters
Percentage reabsorbed>99%

Filtration Pressures

Glomerular hydrostatic pressure (PGC)~60 mmHg (favors filtration)
Capsular hydrostatic pressure (PBC)~15 mmHg (opposes filtration)
Blood colloid osmotic pressure (πGC)~28 mmHg (opposes filtration)
Net Filtration Pressure (NFP)~17 mmHg
NFP = PGC − PBC − πGC
Net Filtration Pressure = 60 − 15 − 28 ≈ 17 mmHg (always favors filtration)

🏥 Why is Glomerular Pressure So High?

The afferent arteriole is wide (low resistance in), but the efferent arteriole is narrow (high resistance out). This "backs up" blood in the glomerulus, maintaining ~60 mmHg pressure—much higher than typical capillaries (~20 mmHg). This high pressure is essential for effective filtration.

5Regulation of GFR

💡 The Big Idea

GFR autoregulation maintains relatively constant filtration despite changes in blood pressure (within 80-180 mmHg). This prevents excessive fluid loss or inadequate waste removal.

🔑 GFR Autoregulation Mechanisms

  • Myogenic mechanism: Afferent arteriole smooth muscle responds to stretch
    • ↑ Blood pressure → stretch → afferent arteriole constricts → GFR maintained
    • ↓ Blood pressure → less stretch → afferent arteriole dilates → GFR maintained
    • Works in seconds
  • Tubuloglomerular feedback: Macula densa senses NaCl concentration
    • High NaCl (= high GFR) → release vasoconstrictors → afferent constriction → ↓GFR
    • Low NaCl (= low GFR) → afferent dilation + renin release → ↑GFR

🏥 Clinical Connection: Severe Hemorrhage

During severe blood loss, strong sympathetic stimulation overrides autoregulation and causes intense renal vasoconstriction. This dramatically reduces GFR and urine output, conserving fluid to maintain blood pressure to vital organs (brain, heart).

6Tubular Processing

Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)

The PCT is the "workhorse" of reabsorption. PCT cells have abundant microvilli (brush border) to increase surface area for reabsorption.

PCT Reabsorption

Filtrate volume reabsorbed~65%
Glucose reabsorbed~100%
Amino acids reabsorbed~100%
Na⁺ reabsorbed~65%
Minimum daily urine output~440 mL

🔑 Water Reabsorption in PCT

Water follows solutes by osmosis. After passing through the PCT, filtrate is still isotonic (same concentration as plasma)—solutes and water are reabsorbed proportionally.

Sodium Transport Mechanisms

Understanding WHERE transporters are located is critical:

  • Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump: Located on basolateral membrane of all tubule cells. Actively pumps 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in—creates the gradient that drives most reabsorption
  • PCT apical membrane: Na⁺ enters via secondary active transport (Na⁺-glucose cotransporters SGLT on apical membrane, Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers)
  • DCT/CD apical membrane: Na⁺ enters through sodium channels (ENaC)—regulated by aldosterone

Key point: Na⁺ is actively transported across the basolateral membrane by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump in BOTH proximal and distal tubules.

🔑 Proximal Tubule Bicarbonate Handling

Carbonic anhydrase on the PCT apical membrane is essential for HCO₃⁻ reabsorption. Process:

  1. H⁺ is secreted via Na⁺/H⁺ exchangers (and H⁺ primary active transporters)
  2. H⁺ combines with filtered HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O (catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase)
  3. CO₂ diffuses into the cell, reforms HCO₃⁻
  4. HCO₃⁻ exits via basolateral membrane through Na⁺/HCO₃⁻ cotransporters and HCO₃⁻/Cl⁻ exchangers

Under severe acidic conditions, proximal tubules can convert glutamine into new bicarbonate.

Loop of Henle

🔑 Countercurrent Multiplier

The Loop of Henle creates the medullary osmotic gradient (300→1200 mOsm):

  • Descending limb: Permeable to water, impermeable to solutes. Water leaves → filtrate concentrates (up to 1200 mOsm at hairpin)
  • Ascending limb: Permeable to solutes, impermeable to water. NaCl pumped out → filtrate dilutes (~100 mOsm)

🔑 Thick Ascending Limb

Contains Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporters that pump solutes OUT while being impermeable to water. This:

  • Dilutes the filtrate (creating "diluting segment")
  • Establishes and maintains the medullary osmotic gradient

Urea also contributes to maintaining the medullary interstitial gradient.

🔑 Vasa Recta Function

The vasa recta are capillaries that follow the loop of Henle. They preserve the medullary gradient while removing reabsorbed water—acting as countercurrent exchangers rather than washing out the gradient.

🏥 Clinical Connection: Glucosuria in Diabetes

Glucose transporters have a maximum capacity—the transport maximum (Tm) ≈ 375 mg/min. In uncontrolled diabetes, blood glucose exceeds Tm, and excess glucose spills into urine—a classic sign of diabetes mellitus.

💡 Without ADH: Dilute Urine

If nothing else is removed from filtrate after the late distal tubule, the urine would be low osmolarity (dilute) and large volume. ADH is required to concentrate urine by allowing water reabsorption in the collecting duct.

7Principal & Intercalated Cells

💡 The Big Idea

The late distal tubule and collecting duct contain two specialized cell types: principal cells regulate water and Na⁺/K⁺ balance, while intercalated cells regulate acid-base balance. This is where hormonal regulation primarily occurs.

🔑 Principal Cells (Water & Electrolyte Balance)

  • ADH receptors: Located on the basolateral membrane
  • ADH mechanism: Binds V2 receptor → activates cAMP second messenger system → vesicles containing aquaporin-2 fuse with apical membrane
  • Aquaporin-2: Inserted into apical membrane when ADH present (regulated)
  • Aquaporin-3: Always present on basolateral membrane (constitutive—present even without ADH)
  • ADH also increases synthesis of sodium channels in principal cells (not just water permeability!)

🔑 Aldosterone Effects on Principal Cells

  • Increases Na⁺ channels (ENaC) on apical membrane → more Na⁺ reabsorption
  • Increases Na⁺/K⁺ pumps on basolateral membrane
  • Increases K⁺ channels on apical membrane → more K⁺ secretion

Result: ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption, ↑ K⁺ secretion (this is why aldosterone causes potassium loss!)

🔑 Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP) Effects

ANP opposes aldosterone! Released from atria when stretched by high blood volume.

  • Effect on principal cells: Decreases number of open sodium channels in apical membrane → ↓ Na⁺ reabsorption
  • Result: More Na⁺ and water excreted → lowers blood volume and pressure

Potassium Handling

🔑 K⁺ Reabsorption vs Secretion

  • K⁺ reabsorption (PCT): Most K⁺ reabsorbed via paracellular pathway; K⁺ channels on basolateral membrane needed for reabsorption
  • K⁺ secretion (DCT/CD): Regulated in principal cells; K⁺ channels on apical membrane needed for secretion
  • Aldosterone increases K⁺ secretion by increasing apical K⁺ channels

🔑 Intercalated Cells (Acid-Base Balance)

  • Type A (α) intercalated cells: Secrete H⁺ into tubule, reabsorb HCO₃⁻ (active during acidosis)
  • Type B (β) intercalated cells: Secrete HCO₃⁻ into tubule, reabsorb H⁺ (active during alkalosis)
  • These cells fine-tune blood pH that buffers and respiration cannot fully correct

🏥 Water Movement in Collecting Ducts

Water moves out of the collecting duct by osmosis—following the medullary concentration gradient created by the loop of Henle.

  • High ADH: Aquaporin-2 inserted → water reabsorption maximal → concentrated, low-volume urine
  • Low ADH: Collecting duct impermeable → dilute, high-volume urine

🏥 Clinical Connection: Diabetes Insipidus

Central DI: Hypothalamus/pituitary doesn't produce enough ADH

Nephrogenic DI: Kidneys don't respond to ADH (receptor or aquaporin problem)

Why polyuria occurs: Lack of ADH (or response to it) decreases water reabsorption → massive dilute urine output (up to 20 L/day!)

8Renin & the Juxtaglomerular Apparatus

🔑 Granular (JG) Cells

Renin is released by granular cells (also called juxtaglomerular cells) located in the wall of the afferent arteriole. These cells act as baroreceptors and respond to:

  • ↓ Blood pressure in the afferent arteriole (direct stretch sensing)
  • ↓ NaCl at the macula densa (paracrine signaling)
  • ↑ Sympathetic nervous system activity

🏥 Why This Matters: RAAS Initiation

Renin is the rate-limiting step for the entire RAAS cascade. Understanding that JG cells release renin in response to low blood pressure explains why hypotension triggers sodium and water retention—the body's attempt to restore blood volume and pressure.

1 Describe the three basic renal processes and explain how they work together to form urine. +
Answer:

The three processes are:

  1. Filtration: At the glomerulus, pressure forces water and small solutes (but not proteins or cells) from blood into Bowman's capsule
  2. Reabsorption: Needed substances (glucose, amino acids, water, ions) are transported from tubules back into peritubular capillaries. Most filtrate (>99%) is reclaimed
  3. Secretion: Some substances are transported FROM blood INTO the tubule, allowing removal of substances that weren't filtered (H⁺, K⁺, drug metabolites)

The equation: Amount Excreted = Filtered + Secreted − Reabsorbed

This "filter-then-reclaim" strategy is more efficient than trying to selectively remove specific wastes.

9 A person with cirrhosis of the liver has lower than normal levels of plasma proteins and a higher than normal GFR. Explain why a decrease in plasma protein concentration would cause an increase in GFR. +
Answer:

The primary driving force for GFR is blood pressure, opposed by fluid pressure in Bowman's capsule and osmotic pressure due to plasma proteins. If a person has fewer plasma proteins due to liver disease, the plasma will have a lower osmotic pressure. With less osmotic pressure opposing the GFR, GFR will increase.

Net Filtration Pressure = Glomerular HP - Bowman's HP - Oncotic Pressure

When oncotic pressure decreases, NFP increases, leading to increased GFR.

10 Patrick's urine sample reveals a high concentration of glucose. Is glucose normally present in urine? Suggest two possible mechanisms to explain why the kidney would excrete excess glucose, and what abnormality may underlie those conditions. +
Answer:

Glucose is normally NOT present in urine because the kidneys reabsorb all filtered glucose in healthy individuals.

Two possible mechanisms for glucosuria:

  1. Glucose transporters may be saturated: This prevents complete reabsorption of glucose. This can happen with extremely high glucose ingestion or with untreated diabetes mellitus (most common cause).
  2. Genetic defect: A genetic defect resulting in an insufficient number of glucose transporters is another possibility (renal glucosuria).

In diabetes, blood glucose exceeds the transport maximum (~375 mg/min), causing excess glucose to "spill" into urine.

11 Trace a drop of water through the urinary system, beginning with the plasma in the renal artery. +
Answer:

Plasma in the renal artery flows into smaller branches, entering the arcuate arteries, which lead to the afferent arterioles, which in turn lead to the glomeruli.

Water moves from glomeruli into the nephrons (PCT, loop of Henle, then DCT), into the collecting ducts, then renal calyces and into the renal pelvis.

From there, it passes into a ureter, then to the urinary bladder; finally, it is expelled as urine through the urethra.

12 List and describe the three filtration barriers that substances leaving the plasma must pass through before entering the tubule lumen. +
Answer:

The three filtration barriers are:

  1. The glomerular capillary endothelium: A fenestrated (porous) endothelium with holes that allow most plasma components through but block blood cells
  2. The basal lamina (basement membrane): A protein-polysaccharide gel matrix that blocks large proteins and has a negative charge that repels most plasma proteins
  3. The epithelium of Bowman's capsule (podocytes): Specialized cells with foot processes that wrap around capillaries, creating filtration slits between them

See the "Renal Corpuscle Contains Filtration Barriers" section of the chapter.

13 List and summarize the forces that influence filtration across the walls of glomerular capillaries. +
Answer:

Three Starling forces influence glomerular filtration:

  1. Hydrostatic pressure (glomerular capillary): ~60 mmHg - FAVORS filtration; this is the primary driving force created by blood pressure
  2. Colloid osmotic pressure (plasma proteins): ~28 mmHg - OPPOSES filtration; proteins in blood pull water back
  3. Hydrostatic fluid pressure (Bowman's capsule): ~15 mmHg - OPPOSES filtration; fluid already in Bowman's capsule pushes back

NFP = 60 - 28 - 15 = 17 mmHg (always favors filtration)

See the "Capillary Pressure Causes Filtration" section of the chapter.

14 Reabsorption involves which two methods of transport? Describe each. What determines which route a solute will take? +
Answer:

Reabsorption involves two transport methods:

  1. Transepithelial transport: Substances cross both apical and basolateral membranes of the tubule epithelial cell. This is the route for actively transported substances like glucose and amino acids.
  2. Paracellular pathway: Substances pass through the junction between two adjacent cells. This route is used for passive movement of water and some ions.

What determines the route? The permeability of the epithelial junction and the electrochemical gradient for the solute determines which route it will take. Tight junctions in different nephron segments have different permeabilities.

15 Describe GFR autoregulation, and explain why it is important. +
Answer:

GFR autoregulation maintains constant filtration despite changes in blood pressure (effective within 80-180 mmHg range).

Two mechanisms:

  1. Myogenic response: The ability of vascular smooth muscle to constrict or dilate to keep pressure and flow constant. Vasoconstriction is more effective at this than vasodilation. When the afferent arteriole is stretched by increased BP, it constricts.
  2. Tubuloglomerular feedback: Involves production of a paracrine signal from the macula densa (in response to increased GFR/NaCl), which constricts the afferent arteriole to decrease GFR.

Why important: Autoregulation helps protect the filtration barriers from high blood pressures that could damage them. It also ensures consistent waste removal and prevents excessive fluid/electrolyte loss.

16 Ruby is 77 years old and can no longer control her urination. What changed in the reflex pathway that used to control her urination? +
Answer:

Micturition (urination) normally occurs when stretch receptors in the bladder wall are stimulated by distension due to urine accumulation. The stretch receptors project to an integration center in the spinal cord, which stimulates parasympathetic neurons of the bladder wall.

This causes bladder contraction, forcing the internal urethral sphincter to open. At the same time, the spinal control center inhibits somatic motor neurons that control the external urethral sphincter, which relaxes in response, allowing urine to flow out.

The spinal reflex is normally overridden by descending control from the brain stem and cerebral cortex. The brain inhibits the parasympathetic output to the bladder and stimulates the somatic motor output to the external urethral sphincter, preventing urination until a convenient time.

Ruby's problem: It is likely that Ruby's descending control is what is no longer working properly; thus she is not able to suppress the urge until she can get to a toilet. This is common in aging due to weakened pelvic floor muscles or neurological changes.

17 Sylvia is suffering from severe edema in her arms and legs. Her physician prescribes a diuretic (a substance that will increase the volume of urine produced). Why might this help to alleviate Sylvia's problem? +
Answer:

One simple answer is that a diuretic reduces the free fluid in the body. A more complex explanation is that increasing the volume of urine produced would decrease the total blood volume of the body.

This in turn would lead to a decreased blood hydrostatic pressure. Edema is frequently the result of hydrostatic pressure of the blood exceeding the opposing forces at the capillaries in the affected area.

Depending on the actual cause of the edema, decreasing the blood hydrostatic pressure would:

  • Decrease edema formation
  • Possibly cause some of the fluid to move from the interstitial space back to the blood

Diuresis would also increase the concentration of proteins in the plasma, contributing to the fluid's movement out of the tissues and into the blood.

18 A professor gave the following analogy of kidney function: "Imagine your desk is a disorganized mess. Instead of picking through each item one by one, you sweep your arm across your desktop in one smooth motion, knocking everything into your large trash can. Then you pull the items you want to keep out of the trash can and place them back on your desk." In what ways is this analogy accurate? In what ways is it inaccurate? +
Answer:

Accurate aspects:

  • The glomerulus does "dump" contents into the nephron (like sweeping the desk into the trash)
  • The body takes back what it needs (reabsorption = pulling items out of trash)
  • The "filter first, then reclaim" strategy is correctly represented

Inaccurate aspects:

  • The entire contents of plasma are NOT dumped - only about 1/5 (20%) of the volume enters the nephron (filtration fraction)
  • Some students argue that kidneys are inefficient and poorly designed, but the professor should respond that the kidney can only use the tools it has: membrane transport processes and physical laws governing movement of water and solutes
  • Kidney dialysis machines rely on diffusion through semipermeable membranes, mimicking part of normal kidney function - this shows the design is actually quite elegant
19 Presence of glucose in the urine is evidence of what condition? +
Answer:

Diabetes mellitus

Glucose in the urine (glucosuria) is a classic sign of diabetes mellitus. In this condition, blood glucose levels are chronically elevated, exceeding the kidney's transport maximum for glucose reabsorption (~375 mg/min).

When glucose transporters (SGLT2) become saturated, excess glucose "spills" into the urine. This is why diabetes was historically diagnosed by tasting urine (sweet taste indicated "mellitus" - meaning honey).

20 Armann weighs 100 kg. Assume his total blood volume is 7%, his heart pumps his total blood volume within 90 seconds, and his renal blood flow is 20% of his cardiac output. Calculate the volume of blood that flows through Armann's kidneys each minute. +
Answer:

Step-by-step calculation:

  1. Blood volume = 100 kg × 0.07 = 7 L = 7000 mL
  2. At least two thirds of this volume must circulate per minute = 7000 mL × 0.67 = 4690 mL/min (minimum cardiac output); values up to 5000 mL/min would still be within a normal range
  3. Renal blood flow = 4690 × 20% = 938 mL/min
  4. Using 5000 mL/min: 5000 × 20% = 1000 mL/min

Thus, blood flow to Armann's kidneys approximates 938-1000 mL/min

This represents about 20-25% of cardiac output going to organs that represent less than 1% of body mass - showing how important the kidneys are for blood filtration!

2 Explain the forces involved in glomerular filtration and calculate the net filtration pressure. +
Answer:

Three Starling forces determine filtration:

  1. Glomerular hydrostatic pressure (PGC = 60 mmHg): Blood pressure pushing fluid OUT—FAVORS filtration
  2. Capsular hydrostatic pressure (PBC = 15 mmHg): Fluid in Bowman's capsule pushing back—OPPOSES filtration
  3. Glomerular osmotic pressure (πGC = 28 mmHg): Plasma proteins pulling fluid back—OPPOSES filtration

NFP = PGC − PBC − πGC = 60 − 15 − 28 = 17 mmHg

This positive pressure always favors filtration. Glomerular pressure is unusually high (~60 vs ~20 mmHg in other capillaries) because the efferent arteriole creates resistance that "backs up" pressure.

3 Describe the two mechanisms of GFR autoregulation and explain why maintaining constant GFR is important. +
Answer:

GFR autoregulation maintains constant filtration despite blood pressure changes (within 80-180 mmHg).

Two mechanisms:

  1. Myogenic mechanism: Afferent arteriole smooth muscle responds to stretch. ↑BP → stretch → vasoconstriction → ↓blood flow → GFR maintained. Works in seconds.
  2. Tubuloglomerular feedback: Macula densa cells sense NaCl in tubular fluid. High NaCl (high GFR) → release vasoconstrictors → afferent constriction → ↓GFR

Why it matters: Too high GFR = excessive fluid/solute loss; too low = inadequate waste removal. Without autoregulation, every BP fluctuation would dramatically alter urine output.

4 Compare and contrast what happens in different segments of the renal tubule. +
Answer:

PCT ("workhorse"): Reabsorbs ~65% of filtrate, 100% of glucose/amino acids, ~65% Na⁺. Has microvilli, many mitochondria. Filtrate stays isotonic.

Loop of Henle ("concentrator"): Creates medullary osmotic gradient.

  • Descending: permeable to water only—water leaves, filtrate concentrates (up to 1200 mOsm)
  • Ascending: permeable to solutes only—NaCl pumped out, filtrate dilutes (~100 mOsm)

DCT ("fine-tuning"): Hormone-regulated Na⁺/Ca²⁺ reabsorption and K⁺/H⁺ secretion.

Collecting Duct ("final decision"): ADH determines water permeability. With ADH → concentrated urine; without ADH → dilute urine. Aldosterone controls Na⁺/K⁺.

5 Explain how the countercurrent multiplier system works to concentrate urine. +
Answer:

The countercurrent multiplier creates the medullary osmotic gradient (300→1200 mOsm) essential for concentrating urine.

How it works:

  1. Descending and ascending limbs have opposite permeabilities and flow in opposite directions
  2. Descending limb: permeable to water, not solutes. Water leaves into hypertonic medulla—filtrate concentrates
  3. Ascending limb: permeable to solutes, not water. NaCl pumped out—filtrate dilutes
  4. The NaCl pumped out by ascending limb creates the medullary concentration that draws water from descending limb
  5. Vasa recta maintain the gradient rather than washing it away

The concentrated medulla then allows the collecting duct to reabsorb water when ADH is present.

6 Explain glucose reabsorption in the kidney. What is transport maximum (Tm) and what causes glucosuria? +
Answer:

Glucose is freely filtered and normally 100% reabsorbed in the PCT via Na⁺-glucose cotransporters (SGLT2).

However, these transporters have a maximum capacity—the transport maximum (Tm) ≈ 375 mg/min.

In healthy individuals, blood glucose (~100 mg/dL) creates a filtered load well below Tm, so all glucose is reclaimed.

In uncontrolled diabetes:

  • Blood glucose is very high (often >300 mg/dL)
  • Filtered glucose exceeds Tm
  • Excess glucose "spills" into urine = glucosuria
  • Glucose creates osmotic diuresis → polyuria (excessive urination) and polydipsia (excessive thirst)
7 Compare cortical and juxtamedullary nephrons. Why are juxtamedullary nephrons important? +
Answer:

Cortical nephrons (80-85%): Short loops of Henle, barely penetrate medulla. Perform standard filtration.

Juxtamedullary nephrons (15-20%): Long loops extending deep into inner medulla. Glomeruli near cortex-medulla junction.

Why juxtamedullary nephrons matter:

  • Their long loops are essential for creating the medullary osmotic gradient through countercurrent multiplication
  • Without them, we couldn't produce concentrated urine
  • Associated vasa recta act as countercurrent exchangers, maintaining the gradient

This is why humans can concentrate urine up to 1200 mOsm—desert animals with even longer loops can reach 5000 mOsm!

8 Why does the kidney have two capillary beds in series? How does this arrangement benefit kidney function? +
Answer:

The kidney's unique arrangement (glomerular capillaries → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries) optimizes both filtration and reabsorption:

  1. Glomerular capillaries: HIGH pressure (~60 mmHg) between two arterioles. Narrow efferent arteriole creates resistance. High pressure drives FILTRATION.
  2. Peritubular capillaries: LOW pressure (~10 mmHg) with high oncotic pressure. These conditions favor REABSORPTION—pulling water/solutes back from tubules.

Key advantage: Connecting beds with an arteriole (not venule) allows independent pressure control:

  • Constricting afferent arteriole → ↓both GFR and peritubular flow
  • Constricting efferent arteriole → ↑GFR but ↓peritubular flow

The body can fine-tune filtration and reabsorption independently.
