1Body Fluid Compartments

Your body is about 60% water (for an average adult male). This water isn't just sloshing around randomly—it's organized into distinct compartments, and the balance between them is critical for proper cell function.

💡 The Big Idea

Water moves freely between compartments by osmosis, always flowing toward higher solute concentration. By controlling SOLUTE concentrations (especially Na⁺), the body indirectly controls WATER distribution.

The Three Fluid Compartments

Fluid Distribution (70 kg adult male)

Total Body Water (TBW) ~40 L (60%)
Intracellular Fluid (ICF) — inside cells ~25 L (62%)
Extracellular Fluid (ECF) — outside cells ~15 L (38%)
• Interstitial fluid (between cells) ~12 L (30%)
• Plasma (in blood vessels) ~3 L (8%)

Variations in body water:

  • Women have less (~50%) due to more body fat
  • Elderly have less (~45%) due to loss of lean mass
  • Infants have more (~75%) due to higher metabolic needs
  • More fat = less water (adipose tissue is ~10% water)

Electrolyte Distribution: ICF vs ECF

The composition of ICF and ECF is dramatically different, maintained by membrane pumps:

ECF (Extracellular) ICF (Intracellular)
Major cation: Na⁺ (136-145 mEq/L) Major cation: K⁺ (~150 mEq/L)
Major anion: Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻ Major anion: HPO₄²⁻ and proteins
Low K⁺ (3.5-5.1 mEq/L) Low Na⁺ (~10 mEq/L)

🔑 Key Concept: Na⁺ is the Boss of ECF

Because Na⁺ is the dominant ECF solute, sodium determines ECF volume. When you retain Na⁺, water follows by osmosis → ECF expands → blood volume and pressure increase. This is why high-sodium diets contribute to hypertension!

Water Balance: Inputs and Outputs

Your body carefully matches water intake with water loss:

Water Inputs (~2500 mL/day) Water Outputs (~2500 mL/day)
Beverages: ~1500 mL Urine: ~1500 mL
Food: ~750 mL Insensible loss (skin, lungs): ~900 mL
Metabolic water: ~250 mL Sweat: ~100 mL (variable)
Feces: ~100 mL

2Regulation of Water Balance: ADH

The primary regulator of water balance is antidiuretic hormone (ADH), also called vasopressin. ADH is produced by the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary.

💡 The Big Idea

ADH controls how much water the kidneys reabsorb. More ADH = more water reabsorption = concentrated urine. Less ADH = less water reabsorption = dilute urine. It's like adjusting a faucet on your kidney's water recycling system.

How ADH Works

ADH Response to Dehydration

1
Stimulus: ↑ Plasma osmolarity (or ↓ blood volume)

Losing water (sweating, not drinking) concentrates blood solutes

2
Detection: Osmoreceptors in hypothalamus

These neurons shrink when plasma is hypertonic, triggering ADH release

3
Response: ↑ ADH secretion from posterior pituitary

ADH travels via blood to its target: kidney collecting ducts

4
Effect: Aquaporin insertion → ↑ water reabsorption

ADH triggers insertion of water channels (aquaporins) into collecting duct cells

5
Result: Concentrated urine, water conserved

More water returns to blood → plasma osmolarity decreases → negative feedback complete

ADH Negative Feedback Loop
↑ Plasma osmolarity → Osmoreceptors detect → ↑ ADH release
↓
↑ Water reabsorption → ↓ Plasma osmolarity → Negative feedback

The Thirst Mechanism

ADH conserves water you already have, but to gain water you need to drink. Three signals trigger thirst:

  1. ↑ Plasma osmolarity → Osmoreceptors in hypothalamus
  2. ↓ Blood volume → Angiotensin II stimulates thirst center
  3. Dry mouth/throat → Sensory signals to brain

🏥 Why Thirst Gets Suppressed Before Full Rehydration

You stop feeling thirsty after drinking even before the water is absorbed and corrects osmolarity. This "anticipatory" response from mouth/stomach stretch receptors prevents overdrinking!

🏥 Clinical Connection: Diabetes Insipidus

Not to be confused with diabetes mellitus! In diabetes insipidus, either ADH isn't produced (central DI) or kidneys don't respond to it (nephrogenic DI). Result: massive amounts of dilute urine (up to 20 L/day!) and extreme thirst. Without treatment, severe dehydration can occur rapidly.

3Sodium Balance: Aldosterone & RAAS

While ADH regulates water, aldosterone is the primary regulator of sodium. Since sodium determines ECF volume, aldosterone indirectly controls blood volume and pressure.

💡 The Big Idea

Aldosterone tells the kidneys: "Save sodium!" It acts on principal cells of the collecting duct to increase Na⁺ reabsorption (and K⁺ secretion). When you save sodium, water follows—increasing blood volume and pressure.

Aldosterone Effects

  • ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption (DCT and collecting duct)
  • ↑ K⁺ secretion (trades Na⁺ for K⁺)
  • ↑ Water reabsorption (follows Na⁺ if ADH is present)
  • Net result: ↑ blood volume → ↑ blood pressure

The Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)

RAAS is a powerful hormonal cascade activated by low blood pressure or low blood volume:

RAAS Pathway

1
↓ Blood pressure detected by JG cells

Juxtaglomerular cells in the kidney sense reduced stretch in afferent arterioles

2
Renin released into blood

Renin is an enzyme that starts the cascade

3
Renin converts angiotensinogen → Angiotensin I

Angiotensinogen is made by the liver and circulates in blood

4
ACE converts Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II

ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) is found mainly in lung capillaries

5
Angiotensin II has multiple effects:

• Vasoconstriction → ↑ blood pressure immediately
• Stimulates aldosterone release → Na⁺/water retention
• Stimulates ADH release → water retention
• Stimulates thirst → ↑ water intake

RAAS: From Low BP to Restored BP
↓ Blood pressure → JG cells release renin
↓
Angiotensinogen → Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II
↓
Vasoconstriction + Aldosterone + ADH + Thirst
↓
↑ Blood pressure restored

🏥 Clinical Connection: ACE Inhibitors

Drugs like lisinopril and enalapril block ACE, preventing Angiotensin II formation. This reduces vasoconstriction and aldosterone release → lower blood pressure. They're first-line treatment for hypertension and heart failure. Side effect: dry cough (due to bradykinin accumulation).

🤔 Check Your Understanding

A patient takes a diuretic that blocks Na⁺ reabsorption. What happens to their K⁺ levels and why?

K⁺ levels may drop (hypokalemia). When less Na⁺ is reabsorbed proximally, more reaches the collecting duct. The body compensates by increasing aldosterone, which enhances Na⁺ reabsorption there—but also increases K⁺ secretion. More K⁺ is lost in urine. This is why some diuretics are called "potassium-wasting" and why patients may need K⁺ supplements.

4Acid-Base Balance: Why pH Matters

Your blood pH must stay within a very narrow range: 7.35-7.45. Even small deviations can be life-threatening because pH affects protein shape and enzyme function.

💡 The Big Idea

pH is maintained by a balance between CO₂ (controlled by lungs) and HCO₃⁻ (controlled by kidneys). Think of it as a seesaw: lungs control one side, kidneys control the other. The ratio must stay at 20:1 for pH to be normal.

Normal Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) Values

pH 7.35 - 7.45
PCO₂ (respiratory component) 35 - 45 mmHg
HCO₃⁻ (metabolic component) 22 - 26 mEq/L

The Key Equation: Bicarbonate Buffer System

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺
This equilibrium is the foundation of blood pH regulation

Understanding this equation is crucial:

  • ↑ CO₂ → reaction shifts RIGHT → ↑ H⁺ → ↓ pH (acidosis)
  • ↓ CO₂ → reaction shifts LEFT → ↓ H⁺ → ↑ pH (alkalosis)
  • ↑ HCO₃⁻ → buffers H⁺ → ↑ pH (alkalosis)
  • ↓ HCO₃⁻ → less buffering → ↓ pH (acidosis)

The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻]/[CO₂])
For pH = 7.4, the ratio [HCO₃⁻]:[CO₂] must equal 20:1
The pH Seesaw: Maintaining 20:1 Balance
🫁 Lungs
CO₂ (PCO₂)
= 1
⚖️
🫘 Kidneys
HCO₃⁻
= 20
[HCO₃⁻] : [CO₂] = 20 : 1 → pH = 7.4

It's the RATIO that matters, not the absolute values!

Three Lines of Defense Against pH Changes

  1. Chemical Buffers (seconds)
    • Bicarbonate buffer: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ H₂CO₃ (most important in blood)
    • Phosphate buffer: HPO₄²⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ H₂PO₄⁻ (important inside cells, urine)
    • Protein buffers: Including hemoglobin
  2. Respiratory Compensation (minutes)
    • ↑ ventilation → ↓ CO₂ → ↑ pH
    • ↓ ventilation → ↑ CO₂ → ↓ pH
  3. Renal Compensation (hours to days)
    • Kidneys can excrete or retain H⁺
    • Kidneys can excrete or retain HCO₃⁻
    • Kidneys can generate NEW bicarbonate

💭 Think of the 20:1 ratio like a seesaw...

If one side gets heavier (more CO₂ or less HCO₃⁻), the seesaw tips toward acidosis. If the other side gets heavier (less CO₂ or more HCO₃⁻), it tips toward alkalosis. The body's goal is always to level the seesaw back to 20:1. The healthy organ compensates for the sick one!

5The Four Acid-Base Disorders

There are only four primary acid-base disorders. The key to understanding them is knowing which organ is causing the problem (lungs = respiratory, kidneys = metabolic) and which way pH has shifted.

Disorder pH Primary Problem Compensation
Respiratory Acidosis < 7.35 ↑ PCO₂ (hypoventilation) Kidneys ↑ HCO₃⁻
Respiratory Alkalosis > 7.45 ↓ PCO₂ (hyperventilation) Kidneys ↓ HCO₃⁻
Metabolic Acidosis < 7.35 ↓ HCO₃⁻ or ↑ H⁺ Lungs ↓ PCO₂ (hyperventilation)
Metabolic Alkalosis > 7.45 ↑ HCO₃⁻ or ↓ H⁺ Lungs ↑ PCO₂ (hypoventilation)

🔑 Key Concept: Cross-System Compensation

Metabolic problems → Respiratory compensation (fast)
Respiratory problems → Renal compensation (slow)

The healthy organ tries to restore the 20:1 ratio. Lungs can adjust CO₂ in minutes; kidneys need hours to days to adjust HCO₃⁻.

Respiratory Acidosis

Cause: Hypoventilation → CO₂ accumulates

Examples: COPD, drug overdose (opioids), airway obstruction, respiratory muscle weakness

Hypoventilation → ↑ PCO₂ → ↑ H⁺ → ↓ pH
Kidneys compensate: ↑ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption

Respiratory Alkalosis

Cause: Hyperventilation → CO₂ blown off

Examples: Anxiety/panic, high altitude, fever, early stages of aspirin overdose

Hyperventilation → ↓ PCO₂ → ↓ H⁺ → ↑ pH
Kidneys compensate: ↓ HCO₃⁻ (excrete in urine)

Metabolic Acidosis

Cause: Gain of acid (not CO₂) OR loss of bicarbonate

Examples: Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), lactic acidosis, renal failure, severe diarrhea (HCO₃⁻ loss)

↑ Acid or ↓ HCO₃⁻ → ↓ pH
Lungs compensate: Hyperventilation → ↓ PCO₂

Classic sign: Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid breathing) — the body desperately trying to blow off CO₂ to raise pH.

Metabolic Alkalosis

Cause: Loss of acid OR gain of bicarbonate

Examples: Prolonged vomiting (loss of HCl from stomach), antacid overuse, excessive diuretics

↓ Acid or ↑ HCO₃⁻ → ↑ pH
Lungs compensate: Hypoventilation → ↑ PCO₂

Note: Respiratory compensation for metabolic alkalosis is limited—the body won't hypoventilate so much that it becomes hypoxic.

🔬 Deep Dive: How to Read an ABG (Arterial Blood Gas)

▼

Step 1: Look at pH

  • < 7.35 = Acidosis
  • > 7.45 = Alkalosis

Step 2: Identify the primary cause

  • Is PCO₂ abnormal? (Normal: 35-45 mmHg) → Respiratory problem
  • Is HCO₃⁻ abnormal? (Normal: 22-26 mEq/L) → Metabolic problem

Step 3: Check for compensation

  • Is the other value moving in the direction that would correct pH?
  • If yes, compensation is occurring

Example: pH = 7.30, PCO₂ = 60 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ = 28 mEq/L

→ Acidosis (pH < 7.35) + ↑ PCO₂ = Respiratory acidosis

→ HCO₃⁻ is elevated = kidneys are compensating by retaining bicarbonate

🤔 Check Your Understanding

A patient with uncontrolled diabetes has pH = 7.28, PCO₂ = 28 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ = 12 mEq/L. What's the diagnosis and is there compensation?

Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation. The low pH (acidosis) with low HCO₃⁻ (metabolic cause—likely ketoacids from diabetes) indicates metabolic acidosis. The low PCO₂ shows the lungs are hyperventilating to compensate (blowing off CO₂ to reduce the acid load). This is classic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)!

6Renal Acid-Base Regulation

The kidneys are the slow but powerful regulators of acid-base balance. They have three main mechanisms:

1. Bicarbonate Reabsorption

The kidneys reabsorb nearly all filtered HCO₃⁻ (normally ~4,320 mEq/day). This occurs mainly in the PCT and involves carbonic anhydrase (CA):

  • H⁺ is secreted into tubular fluid
  • H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O (in tubular lumen)
  • CO₂ diffuses into cell, reconverted to HCO₃⁻, returned to blood

2. H⁺ Secretion

Intercalated cells of the collecting duct actively secrete H⁺ into urine. This H⁺ is buffered in urine by:

  • Phosphate buffer: HPO₄²⁻ + H⁺ → H₂PO₄⁻ (excreted)
  • Ammonia buffer: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ (excreted as ammonium)

3. New Bicarbonate Generation

In acidosis, the kidneys can manufacture NEW bicarbonate (not just reclaim filtered HCO₃⁻) through glutamine metabolism, adding to the body's buffer capacity.

🔑 Summary: Kidneys in Acidosis vs Alkalosis

In Acidosis (need to ↑ pH) In Alkalosis (need to ↓ pH)
↑ H⁺ secretion ↓ H⁺ secretion
↑ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption ↓ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption (excrete in urine)
↑ New HCO₃⁻ synthesis ↓ New HCO₃⁻ synthesis
Acidic urine produced Alkaline urine produced

📋 Chapter 19 Summary: What You Should Know

  • Body water (~60%) is distributed into ICF (2/3) and ECF (1/3: interstitial + plasma)
  • Na⁺ is the main ECF cation and determines ECF volume; K⁺ is the main ICF cation
  • ADH (from posterior pituitary) increases water reabsorption via aquaporins when osmolarity is high
  • Thirst is triggered by ↑ osmolarity, ↓ blood volume (via angiotensin II), and dry mouth
  • Aldosterone (from adrenal cortex) increases Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ secretion
  • RAAS: ↓ BP → renin → angiotensin I → (ACE) → angiotensin II → vasoconstriction + aldosterone + ADH + thirst
  • Normal blood pH = 7.35-7.45; maintained by [HCO₃⁻]:[CO₂] = 20:1 ratio
  • Buffers act in seconds, respiratory compensation in minutes, renal compensation in hours to days
  • Respiratory acidosis: ↑ PCO₂ (hypoventilation) → kidneys compensate by ↑ HCO₃⁻
  • Respiratory alkalosis: ↓ PCO₂ (hyperventilation) → kidneys compensate by ↓ HCO₃⁻
  • Metabolic acidosis: ↓ HCO₃⁻ or ↑ H⁺ → lungs compensate by hyperventilation (↓ PCO₂)
  • Metabolic alkalosis: ↑ HCO₃⁻ or ↓ H⁺ → lungs compensate by hypoventilation (↑ PCO₂)
  • Kidneys regulate pH by reabsorbing HCO₃⁻, secreting H⁺, and generating new bicarbonate
