Fluid, Electrolyte & Acid-Base Balance

Maintaining the internal environment for cellular life

1Body Fluid Compartments

💡 The Big Idea

Water moves freely between compartments by osmosis, always flowing toward higher solute concentration. By controlling SOLUTE concentrations (especially Na⁺), the body indirectly controls WATER distribution.

Fluid Distribution (70 kg adult male)

Total Body Water (TBW)~40 L (60%)
Intracellular Fluid (ICF)~25 L (62%)
Extracellular Fluid (ECF)~15 L (38%)

🔑 Key Concept: Na⁺ is the Boss of ECF

Because Na⁺ is the dominant ECF solute, sodium determines ECF volume. When you retain Na⁺, water follows → ECF expands → blood volume and pressure increase. This is why high-sodium diets contribute to hypertension!

2Regulation of Water Balance: ADH

💡 The Big Idea

ADH controls how much water the kidneys reabsorb. More ADH = more water reabsorption = concentrated urine. Less ADH = dilute urine. It's like adjusting a faucet on your kidney's water recycling system.

🏥 Clinical Connection: Diabetes Insipidus

Not to be confused with diabetes mellitus! In diabetes insipidus, either ADH isn't produced (central DI) or kidneys don't respond to it (nephrogenic DI). Result: massive amounts of dilute urine (up to 20 L/day!) and extreme thirst.

3Sodium Balance: Aldosterone & RAAS

💡 The Big Idea

Aldosterone tells the kidneys: "Save sodium!" It acts on principal cells of the collecting duct to increase Na⁺ reabsorption (and K⁺ secretion). When you save sodium, water follows—increasing blood volume and pressure.

🔑 The RAAS Pathway

  • ↓ Blood pressure → JG cells release renin
  • Renin converts angiotensinogen → Angiotensin I
  • ACE converts Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II
  • Angiotensin II: Vasoconstriction + Aldosterone + ADH + Thirst

🏥 Clinical Connection: ACE Inhibitors

Drugs like lisinopril block ACE, preventing Angiotensin II formation. This reduces vasoconstriction and aldosterone release → lower blood pressure. They're first-line treatment for hypertension. Side effect: dry cough (bradykinin accumulation).

4Acid-Base Balance: Why pH Matters

💡 The Big Idea

pH is maintained by a balance between CO₂ (controlled by lungs) and HCO₃⁻ (controlled by kidneys). Think of it as a seesaw: lungs control one side, kidneys control the other. The ratio must stay at 20:1 for pH to be normal.

Normal Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) Values

pH7.35 - 7.45
PCO₂ (respiratory component)35 - 45 mmHg
HCO₃⁻ (metabolic component)22 - 26 mEq/L
CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺
This equilibrium is the foundation of blood pH regulation
pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻]/[CO₂])
Henderson-Hasselbalch: For pH = 7.4, the ratio must equal 20:1

5The Four Acid-Base Disorders

Disorder pH Primary Problem Compensation
Respiratory Acidosis < 7.35 ↑ PCO₂ (hypoventilation) Kidneys ↑ HCO₃⁻
Respiratory Alkalosis > 7.45 ↓ PCO₂ (hyperventilation) Kidneys ↓ HCO₃⁻
Metabolic Acidosis < 7.35 ↓ HCO₃⁻ or ↑ H⁺ Lungs ↓ PCO₂
Metabolic Alkalosis > 7.45 ↑ HCO₃⁻ or ↓ H⁺ Lungs ↑ PCO₂

🔑 Key Concept: Cross-System Compensation

Metabolic problems → Respiratory compensation (fast)
Respiratory problems → Renal compensation (slow)
The healthy organ tries to restore the 20:1 ratio!

📋 Chapter 19 Summary

  • Body water (~60%) is distributed into ICF (2/3) and ECF (1/3)
  • Na⁺ is the main ECF cation and determines ECF volume
  • ADH increases water reabsorption via aquaporins when osmolarity is high
  • Aldosterone increases Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ secretion
  • RAAS: ↓ BP → renin → Ang I → ACE → Ang II → vasoconstriction + aldosterone + ADH + thirst
  • Normal blood pH = 7.35-7.45; maintained by [HCO₃⁻]:[CO₂] = 20:1 ratio
  • Respiratory acidosis: ↑ PCO₂ → kidneys compensate by ↑ HCO₃⁻
  • Respiratory alkalosis: ↓ PCO₂ → kidneys compensate by ↓ HCO₃⁻
  • Metabolic acidosis: ↓ HCO₃⁻ → lungs compensate by hyperventilation (↓ PCO₂)
  • Metabolic alkalosis: ↑ HCO₃⁻ → lungs compensate by hypoventilation (↑ PCO₂)

1 Describe the major body fluid compartments and explain why Na⁺ is called "the boss of ECF." +
Answer:

Body water (~60% of body weight, ~40L) is distributed into:

  • Intracellular fluid (ICF, ~25L, 2/3): Inside cells, main cation is K⁺
  • Extracellular fluid (ECF, ~15L, 1/3): Outside cells, subdivided into interstitial fluid (~12L) and plasma (~3L), main cation is Na⁺

Na⁺ is "the boss of ECF" because it's the dominant ECF solute and therefore determines ECF osmolarity. Water moves by osmosis toward higher solute concentration, so controlling Na⁺ effectively controls water distribution.

Retain Na⁺ → water follows → ECF expands → ↑blood volume → ↑blood pressure. This is why high-sodium diets contribute to hypertension.

2 Explain how ADH regulates water balance. Include the stimulus, receptor, and mechanism of action. +
Answer:

ADH (antidiuretic hormone) is the primary regulator of water balance:

  • Stimulus: ↑plasma osmolarity (dehydration) or ↓blood volume/pressure
  • Detection: Osmoreceptors in hypothalamus; baroreceptors detect volume changes
  • Production: Synthesized in hypothalamus, released from posterior pituitary
  • Mechanism: ADH binds V2 receptors on collecting duct → triggers insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels
  • Effect: Water moves from tubule into hypertonic medulla → water reabsorbed → concentrated urine → ↓plasma osmolarity → negative feedback

Without ADH, collecting ducts are impermeable → dilute urine (up to 20L/day in diabetes insipidus).

3 Trace the complete RAAS pathway from stimulus to effects. Why is this system clinically important? +
Answer:

RAAS pathway:

  1. Stimulus: ↓blood pressure, ↓blood volume, or ↓Na⁺ at macula densa
  2. JG cells release renin
  3. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen (liver) → Angiotensin I
  4. ACE (lung capillaries) converts Ang I → Angiotensin II
  5. Ang II effects: Vasoconstriction (↑BP immediately), stimulates aldosterone (↑Na⁺ reabsorption), stimulates ADH (↑water), stimulates thirst

Clinical importance: RAAS is a major drug target for hypertension and heart failure:

  • ACE inhibitors (lisinopril) block Ang II formation
  • ARBs (losartan) block Ang II receptors
  • Understanding RAAS explains drug mechanisms and side effects
4 Explain the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation and why the 20:1 ratio is critical for pH maintenance. +
Answer:

Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻]/[CO₂])

For normal pH of 7.4: the ratio [HCO₃⁻]:[CO₂] must equal 20:1

The critical insight: it's the RATIO, not absolute values, that determines pH.

  • HCO₃⁻ = "metabolic component"—controlled by kidneys (slow, hours-days)
  • CO₂ = "respiratory component"—controlled by lungs (fast, minutes)

This creates a seesaw: if CO₂ rises, kidneys can raise HCO₃⁻ to maintain 20:1. If HCO₃⁻ falls, lungs can blow off CO₂. The 20:1 ratio provides a framework for understanding all acid-base disorders.

5 Compare and contrast the four primary acid-base disorders. Include causes and compensation. +
Answer:

Respiratory Acidosis (pH↓, PCO₂↑): Hypoventilation (COPD, drug overdose). Compensation: kidneys retain HCO₃⁻ (slow).

Respiratory Alkalosis (pH↑, PCO₂↓): Hyperventilation (anxiety, high altitude). Compensation: kidneys excrete HCO₃⁻ (slow).

Metabolic Acidosis (pH↓, HCO₃⁻↓): Acid gain (DKA, lactic acidosis) or HCO₃⁻ loss (diarrhea). Compensation: hyperventilation to ↓CO₂ (fast)—Kussmaul breathing.

Metabolic Alkalosis (pH↑, HCO₃⁻↑): Acid loss (vomiting) or HCO₃⁻ gain (antacids). Compensation: hypoventilation to ↑CO₂ (limited).

Key principle: Respiratory problems → renal compensation (slow); Metabolic problems → respiratory compensation (fast).

6 Describe the steps to interpret an arterial blood gas (ABG) result. +
Answer:

Step 1—Look at pH:

  • <7.35 = acidemia
  • >7.45 = alkalemia

Step 2—Identify primary cause:

  • ↑PCO₂ with ↓pH = respiratory acidosis
  • ↓PCO₂ with ↑pH = respiratory alkalosis
  • ↓HCO₃⁻ with ↓pH = metabolic acidosis
  • ↑HCO₃⁻ with ↑pH = metabolic alkalosis

Step 3—Check for compensation: Is the other value moving to correct pH?

Example: pH 7.30, PCO₂ 60, HCO₃⁻ 28 → Acidosis + ↑PCO₂ = respiratory acidosis. ↑HCO₃⁻ shows renal compensation.

7 Explain the three mechanisms kidneys use to regulate acid-base balance. +
Answer:

1. Bicarbonate reabsorption: ~4320 mEq HCO₃⁻ filtered daily; nearly all reclaimed in PCT. H⁺ secreted → combines with HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O → CO₂ enters cell → reconverted to HCO₃⁻ → returned to blood.

2. H⁺ secretion: Intercalated cells actively secrete H⁺. Buffered in urine by:

  • Phosphate buffer: HPO₄²⁻ + H⁺ → H₂PO₄⁻
  • Ammonia buffer: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺

3. New bicarbonate generation: During acidosis, kidneys metabolize glutamine to produce NH₄⁺ (excreted) and NEW HCO₃⁻ (returned to blood).

In acidosis: ↑H⁺ secretion, ↑HCO₃⁻ reabsorption, ↑new HCO₃⁻ → acidic urine. Opposite in alkalosis.

8 Compare ADH and aldosterone: triggers, targets, and effects on urine composition. +
Answer:

ADH (Antidiuretic Hormone):

  • Trigger: ↑plasma osmolarity, ↓blood volume
  • Source: Hypothalamus → posterior pituitary
  • Target: Collecting duct principal cells
  • Effect: Inserts aquaporins → ↑water reabsorption → ↓urine volume, ↑concentration

Aldosterone:

  • Trigger: ↑Angiotensin II, ↑K⁺, ↓Na⁺
  • Source: Adrenal cortex
  • Target: DCT and collecting duct
  • Effect: ↑Na⁺ reabsorption, ↑K⁺ secretion → water follows Na⁺ (if ADH present)

Key distinction: ADH controls WATER independently; aldosterone controls SODIUM (water follows secondarily).

9 A patient has been vomiting for 3 days. Predict their acid-base status and explain the physiological mechanisms. +
Answer:

Prediction: Metabolic alkalosis with respiratory compensation

Mechanism:

  • Vomiting loses gastric HCl → H⁺ lost → blood becomes alkaline → pH rises
  • Also losing Cl⁻ (hypochloremia) and K⁺
  • HCO₃⁻ that would normally neutralize gastric acid now accumulates
  • Volume depletion activates RAAS → aldosterone → kidney saves Na⁺ but secretes more K⁺ and H⁺ (paradoxically worsening alkalosis)

Compensation: Chemoreceptors detect ↑pH → ↓ventilation → ↑PCO₂ → helps lower pH (but limited—won't become hypoxic)

Expected ABG: pH >7.45, HCO₃⁻ >26, PCO₂ slightly elevated

Treatment: IV normal saline with KCl
