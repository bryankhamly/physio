1The Big Picture: Why Do We Breathe?

Before we dive into anatomy and mechanics, let's understand why breathing exists.

💡 The Big Idea

Every single cell in your body needs oxygen to produce energy (ATP) and produces carbon dioxide as waste. Your respiratory system is the delivery and waste removal service that keeps all 37 trillion cells alive.

🔑 Primary Functions of the Respiratory System

  • Gas exchange: O₂ in, CO₂ out (primary function)
  • Regulation of pH: By controlling CO₂ levels (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻)
  • Protection against pathogens: Mucus, cilia, macrophages filter and trap invaders
  • Vocalization: Air passing over vocal cords produces sound

Note: Regulation of water balance is NOT a primary respiratory function—that's the kidneys!

Your cells perform cellular respiration (oxidative phosphorylation). Cells "burn" glucose using oxygen to produce energy, creating CO₂ as a byproduct.

💭 Think of it like a city...

Imagine your body as a massive city with 37 trillion buildings (cells). Each building needs fresh air delivered and waste hauled away constantly. Your respiratory system is like the ventilation system for the entire city—it brings fresh oxygen in and removes carbon dioxide waste.

Two Types of "Respiration"

Internal Respiration External Respiration
What: Cells using O₂ to make ATP
Where: Inside every cell (mitochondria)		 What: Getting O₂ into blood and CO₂ out
Where: Lungs and blood

2The Pathway: Following Air Through Your Body

💡 The Big Idea

The respiratory system is divided into two zones: the conducting zone (the highway that moves air) and the respiratory zone (where gas exchange happens). Only the respiratory zone can transfer oxygen to your blood.

🔑 Upper vs Lower Respiratory Tract

  • Upper respiratory tract: Nose, nasal cavity, mouth, pharynx, larynx
  • Lower respiratory tract: Trachea + all bronchial branches + lungs

The lungs are located in the thoracic cavity and are surrounded by pleural membranes.

The Air Pathway (in order!)

Nose/Mouth → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Primary bronchi → Secondary bronchi → Bronchioles → Terminal bronchioles → Respiratory bronchioles → Alveolar ducts → Alveoli

Major respiratory organs: nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and lungs

🔑 Key Structures

  • Pharynx: Common passageway shared by respiratory and digestive systems
  • Epiglottis: Prevents food from entering the larynx during swallowing
  • Trachea: Has C-shaped cartilage rings—the open part faces posteriorly, allowing the esophagus to expand during swallowing
  • Right primary bronchus: Shorter, wider, and more vertical than left—aspirated objects more likely to lodge here!
  • Nasal cavity: Warms, humidifies, and filters air via rich blood supply and mucous membranes with cilia

💭 Think of it like an upside-down tree...

The trachea is the trunk, bronchi are major branches, bronchioles are smaller branches, and alveoli are like leaves at the tips. There are about 300 million alveoli!

🔑 Conducting Zone vs Respiratory Zone

  • Conducting zone: Nose to terminal bronchioles. Warms, humidifies, filters air. NO gas exchange. = Anatomical dead space (~150 mL)
  • Respiratory zone: Respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveoli. Gas exchange occurs here!

🔑 Bronchi vs Bronchioles

  • Bronchi: Have cartilage rings in walls (keep them open)
  • Bronchioles: Have smooth muscle but NO cartilage—their diameter can change significantly

This is why asthma affects bronchioles—smooth muscle spasm causes bronchoconstriction!

🔑 The Alveoli: Where Gas Exchange Happens

  • Type I alveolar cells: Thin, flat cells that allow rapid diffusion of gases
  • Type II alveolar cells: Secrete surfactant
  • Alveolar macrophages: Phagocytize particles and pathogens

Alveoli are made of a single layer of simple squamous epithelium + elastin fibers that allow stretching.

🔑 The Respiratory Membrane

Gas exchange occurs across the respiratory membrane, which consists of:

  1. Alveolar epithelium (Type I cells)
  2. Fused basement membranes
  3. Capillary endothelium

Total thickness: only 0.5 μm—allows rapid diffusion!

3How We Breathe: The Physics of Ventilation

💡 The Big Idea

Air flows from HIGH pressure to LOW pressure. To breathe in, you make the pressure inside your lungs LOWER than outside. To breathe out, you make it HIGHER. Your body does this by changing the VOLUME of your chest cavity.

The Gas Laws You Need to Know

Boyle's Law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
At constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related.
↑ Volume = ↓ Pressure | ↓ Volume = ↑ Pressure
Dalton's Law: Ptotal = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + ...
In a gas mixture, total pressure = sum of all partial pressures.
Each gas contributes based on its percentage of the mixture.
Ideal Gas Law: PV = nRT
Relates pressure, volume, temperature, and number of gas molecules.

🔑 Respiratory Muscles

Quiet Inspiration (active):

  • Diaphragm (does ~75% of the work) — innervated by phrenic nerve (C3-C5)
  • External intercostals

Quiet Expiration (passive): Relies on elastic recoil of lungs—no muscle contraction needed!

Forced Expiration (active):

  • Internal intercostals
  • Abdominal muscles

Forced Inspiration: Accessory muscles (sternocleidomastoid, scalenes)

🔑 Autonomic Control of Airway Diameter

  • Parasympathetic stimulation: Bronchoconstriction → increases airway resistance
  • Sympathetic stimulation / Epinephrine / Beta-2 agonists: Bronchodilation → decreases airway resistance

The Three Key Pressures

Atmospheric (Patm) 760 mmHg (= 0)
Intra-alveolar (Palv) Varies: −1 to +1
Intrapleural (Pip) Always negative: ~−4

🔑 Transpulmonary Pressure

Transpulmonary pressure = Intra-alveolar pressure − Intrapleural pressure

This pressure difference keeps the lungs inflated. At rest: 0 − (−4) = +4 mmHg. At end of normal expiration, intra-alveolar pressure equals atmospheric pressure.

🏥 Clinical Connection: Pneumothorax

If the chest wall is punctured, air rushes into the pleural space. The negative intrapleural pressure is lost and the lung collapses. Treatment: chest tube to remove the air and restore negative pressure.

4Lung Volumes & Capacities

🔑 Key Definitions

  • Pulmonary ventilation: Movement of air into and out of the lungs (= breathing)
  • Alveolar ventilation: Movement of air into and out of the alveoli (the air that actually participates in gas exchange)

Lung Volumes (average adult male)

Tidal Volume (VT) — air inhaled/exhaled in normal breath 500 mL
Inspiratory Reserve (IRV) — extra air you CAN inhale 3000 mL
Expiratory Reserve (ERV) — extra air you CAN exhale 1000 mL
Residual Volume (RV) — air that CANNOT be exhaled 1200 mL
Anatomical Dead Space ~150 mL

Lung Capacities (combinations of volumes)

Vital Capacity (VC) = TV + IRV + ERV ~4500 mL
Total Lung Capacity (TLC) = VC + RV ~5700-6000 mL
Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) = ERV + RV ~2200 mL
Spirogram showing lung volumes (TV, IRV, ERV, RV) and capacities (VC, IC, FRC, TLC)
Source: Wikimedia Commons (Public Domain)

🏥 Why Residual Volume Matters

The residual volume prevents the lungs from completely collapsing. Even after maximum exhalation, air remains to keep alveoli open.

Minute Ventilation = Tidal Volume × Respiratory Rate
= 500 mL × 12 breaths/min = 6000 mL/min (total air moved per minute)
Alveolar Ventilation = (VT − Dead Space) × Respiratory Rate
= (500 mL − 150 mL) × 12 breaths/min = 4200 mL/min
Deep, slow breathing improves alveolar ventilation more than shallow, rapid breathing!

🏥 Clinical Connection: Pulmonary Function Tests

FEV₁/FVC ratio helps distinguish lung disease types:

  • Normal: ~80%
  • Obstructive disease (asthma, COPD): <70% (air is trapped, can't get out quickly)
  • Restrictive disease (fibrosis): Normal or increased ratio (both decrease proportionally)

5What Affects How Well You Breathe?

🔑 Lung Compliance

Compliance = the ease with which the lungs can be expanded.

  • High compliance: Lungs expand easily (less work to breathe)
  • Low compliance: Lungs are stiff (more work to breathe)

Pulmonary fibrosis (scarring) causes decreased compliance—lungs become stiff and hard to expand.

🔑 Key Concept: Surfactant

Surfactant is a phospholipid-protein mixture produced by Type II alveolar cells. It reduces surface tension by disrupting hydrogen bonds between water molecules.

Functions:

  • Makes lungs easier to expand (increases compliance)
  • Prevents alveoli from collapsing at the end of expiration
  • Equalizes pressure in different-sized alveoli

🏥 Clinical Connection: Infant Respiratory Distress Syndrome

Premature infants (born before ~28 weeks) often can't produce enough surfactant. This causes:

  • Very high surface tension
  • Alveoli collapse after each breath
  • Each breath requires enormous effort → rapid fatigue

Treatment: artificial surfactant + mechanical ventilation.

🏥 Clinical Connection: Asthma

In asthma, breathing difficulty is caused by bronchoconstriction and inflammation of bronchioles:

  • Smooth muscle spasm narrows airways
  • Inflammation causes swelling
  • Excess mucus production

Treatment: Bronchodilators (albuterol) relax smooth muscle; corticosteroids reduce inflammation.

📋 Chapter 16 Summary

  • Primary functions: gas exchange, pH regulation, protection, vocalization (NOT water balance)
  • Upper tract: nose to larynx; Lower tract: trachea + bronchi + lungs (in thoracic cavity, surrounded by pleural membranes)
  • Air pathway: nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → primary bronchi → secondary bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → alveoli
  • Conducting zone (no gas exchange) = anatomical dead space (~150 mL); Respiratory zone = gas exchange
  • Type I cells = gas diffusion; Type II cells = surfactant; Alveolar macrophages = phagocytosis
  • Bronchi have cartilage; bronchioles have smooth muscle only (can constrict in asthma)
  • Boyle's Law (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂); Dalton's Law (partial pressures); Ideal Gas Law (PV = nRT)
  • Quiet inspiration: diaphragm (phrenic nerve) + external intercostals; Quiet expiration: passive (elastic recoil)
  • Forced expiration: internal intercostals + abdominal muscles
  • Parasympathetic = bronchoconstriction; Sympathetic/epinephrine = bronchodilation
  • TV = 500 mL; VC = 4500 mL; TLC = 5700-6000 mL; RV = 1200 mL (prevents lung collapse)

1 Explain the difference between the conducting zone and the respiratory zone. Why is the conducting zone called "anatomical dead space"? +
Answer:

The conducting zone includes structures from the nose to the terminal bronchioles—its function is to warm, humidify, and filter air, and conduct it to the respiratory zone. NO gas exchange occurs here.

The respiratory zone includes respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and alveoli—this is where actual gas exchange with blood occurs.

The conducting zone is called "anatomical dead space" (~150 mL) because air in this space doesn't participate in gas exchange. This is clinically important because shallow breathing may only move dead space air, leading to inadequate alveolar ventilation.

2 Using Boyle's Law, explain the sequence of events during inspiration. +
Answer:

Boyle's Law states that at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂).

During inspiration:

  1. The diaphragm contracts and flattens; external intercostals lift the ribs up and out
  2. This increases thoracic cavity volume
  3. By Boyle's Law, increased volume causes decreased pressure—intra-alveolar pressure drops below atmospheric pressure (about -1 mmHg)
  4. Air flows from high pressure (atmosphere, 760 mmHg) to low pressure (alveoli, 759 mmHg) until pressures equalize

The reverse occurs during expiration—volume decreases, pressure increases above atmospheric, and air flows out.

3 Why is intrapleural pressure always negative? What happens if air enters the pleural space? +
Answer:

Intrapleural pressure is always negative (about -4 mmHg at rest) due to opposing forces:

  1. The lungs' elastic recoil pulls inward (lungs want to collapse)
  2. The chest wall's elastic recoil pulls outward (chest wants to spring open)
  3. Surface tension in alveoli creates inward pull

These opposing forces create a "suction" effect in the pleural space.

If air enters the pleural space (pneumothorax)—from trauma, rib fracture, or medical procedures—the negative pressure is lost, transpulmonary pressure drops to zero, and the lung collapses. Treatment requires a chest tube to remove air and restore negative pressure.

4 Compare and contrast obstructive and restrictive lung diseases. How does the FEV₁/FVC ratio help distinguish them? +
Answer:

Obstructive diseases (asthma, COPD, chronic bronchitis) involve narrowed airways that increase resistance to airflow—air gets trapped and can't get out quickly.

Restrictive diseases (pulmonary fibrosis, obesity, scoliosis) involve reduced lung compliance—the lungs can't expand fully.

The FEV₁/FVC ratio is key:

  • FEV₁ = air exhaled in the first second of a forced exhalation
  • FVC = total air exhaled
  • Normal ratio is ~80%

In obstructive disease, the ratio is <70% because air is trapped and can't exit quickly (FEV₁ drops more than FVC).

In restrictive disease, the ratio is normal or increased because both FEV₁ and FVC decrease proportionally—the lungs are small but airways are clear.

5 Explain the role of surfactant in the lungs. What happens when surfactant is deficient? +
Answer:

Surfactant is a phospholipid-protein mixture produced by Type II alveolar cells. It reduces surface tension in alveoli, which:

  1. Increases lung compliance (makes lungs easier to expand)
  2. Prevents alveolar collapse at end-expiration
  3. Equalizes pressure in different-sized alveoli

When surfactant is deficient—as in Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) in premature infants born before weeks 24-28:

  • Surface tension is very high
  • Alveoli collapse after each breath
  • Each breath requires enormous effort
  • The infant develops respiratory failure

Treatment: artificial surfactant and mechanical ventilation.

6 Why do bronchioles, rather than bronchi, cause the airway problems in asthma? +
Answer:

Bronchioles have smooth muscle in their walls but NO cartilage, while bronchi have cartilage rings that keep them rigid.

This structural difference is crucial: bronchiole diameter can change significantly when smooth muscle contracts or relaxes, but bronchi remain relatively fixed due to cartilage support.

In asthma, triggers cause:

  1. Bronchiole smooth muscle spasm (bronchoconstriction)
  2. Inflammation and swelling of airway walls
  3. Excess mucus production

All three dramatically narrow the bronchioles, increasing airway resistance and making breathing difficult.

Treatment targets these mechanisms: bronchodilators (like albuterol) relax smooth muscle, and corticosteroids reduce inflammation.
